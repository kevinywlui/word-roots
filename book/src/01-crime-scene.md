# 1. English Is a Crime Scene

Here is a small experiment. Read this sentence:

> The king asked the cook for a hearty meal of bread, meat, and ale.

Now this one:

> The monarch requested a substantial repast of comestibles and beverages.

And this one:

> The potentate solicited nutritive alimentation.

All three mean roughly the same thing. But the first sounds like something a person would say, the second sounds like a hotel menu, and the third sounds like a hostage note written by a pharmacist. The difference is not grammar. The difference is that the first sentence is almost entirely Old English, the second is mostly Norman French (which is to say, Latin that spent a few centuries in France), and the third is Latin borrowed directly, or nearly so, by people who thought Latin was classy.

English has three layers, and every native speaker already feels them, the way you can feel the difference between *ask*, *question*, and *interrogate* without being able to say what it is. This book is about the top two layers. But to understand why they exist, you need the short version of how English got this way.

## Two invasions and a fad

**Layer one: the Germanic core.** The Angles, Saxons, and Jutes brought a West Germanic language to Britain in the fifth century. This layer gives us nearly all of the hundred most common words in English: *the, and, of, I, you, house, hand, eat, sleep, love, water, mother.* If you strip English down to what a toddler knows, it is almost pure Germanic. This is also why English feels so different from French despite sharing so much vocabulary: the skeleton is Germanic, and only the flesh is Latin.

**Layer two: Norman French.** In 1066, William of Normandy conquered England and installed a French-speaking aristocracy. For roughly three hundred years, the people who ran the courts, the church, the army, and the kitchen spoke French, and the people who did the actual work spoke English. You can still hear this. The animal in the field has a Germanic name because the peasant raised it: *cow, pig, sheep, deer.* The meat on the table has a French name because the lord ate it: *beef, pork, mutton, venison.* The Germanic word is *house*; the French word is *mansion*. The Germanic word is *ask*; the French word is *demand*. About ten thousand French words entered English in this period, and roughly three quarters of them are still in use. Since French is a direct descendant of Latin, this is Latin's first big entrance, though it came in disguised, with its consonants softened and its endings worn off.

**Layer three: the Renaissance and the inkhorn.** Starting in the sixteenth century, English scholars who had been educated in Latin and Greek decided English was impoverished and began importing classical words wholesale, straight from the source, no French intermediary. These were called "inkhorn terms" by people who hated them, on the grounds that they smelled of ink rather than speech. Some inkhorn terms died (nobody says *adnichilate* or *fatigate*). Many survived: *dexterity, expensive, encyclopedia, education, celebrate, dismiss.* This is why English so often has a plain French word and a fancier Latin word for the same concept: *frail* and *fragile* are literally the same Latin word, *fragilis*, borrowed twice, once through French in the Middle Ages and once directly in the Renaissance.

Then science happened, and science needed thousands of new words, and the convention was already set: you build them out of Greek and Latin parts. So the top layer kept growing and never stopped. *Television, photosynthesis, antibiotic, cardiovascular, neurotransmitter.* The word *hydrogen* is younger than the United States.

## Why this matters more than it seems

The result is a vocabulary in which the words you learn by living and the words you learn by reading come from different languages. Estimates vary, but a reasonable summary is that around 60 percent of English words have Latin or Greek origins, rising to about 90 percent in scientific and technical vocabulary, while the hundred most frequent words are about 95 percent Germanic.

This is the whole argument for learning roots, so it is worth stating carefully.

Word frequency in any language follows something like a Zipf distribution: a few words appear constantly and a very long tail appears rarely. You learn the head of the distribution for free, by talking to people. Nobody teaches you *the* or *hand*. The tail is different. The tail is where the vocabulary of medicine, law, philosophy, and every academic discipline lives, and the tail is overwhelmingly Latin and Greek. You cannot learn the tail by exposure, because by definition each word in it is rare.

But the tail is not random. It is assembled from a small kit. A few dozen prefixes, a few hundred roots, and a couple dozen suffixes account for an enormous share of it. If you know that *-itis* means inflammation, you do not need to have seen *tendinitis* or *bursitis* or *appendicitis* before; you need to know what a tendon, a bursa, and an appendix are. If you know that *bene-* means well and *dict* means say, then *benediction* is not a word you memorize but a word you compute.

This is unusually good leverage. Most vocabulary study is linear: one word learned, one word known. Root study is multiplicative. Learn *spec* (look) and you have a foothold in *inspect, spectacle, respect, suspect, speculate, perspective, conspicuous, spectrum, species,* and *retrospective*. Learn *-ology* and *-ologist* and you get a free suffix on every field of study that will ever exist.

The rest of this book is that kit, with the interesting parts left in.
