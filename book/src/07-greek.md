# 7. Greek: The Language Science Speaks

Latin got the law, the church, and the administration because Latin was the language of the Roman state and then of the medieval church. Greek got science and medicine for a slightly different reason: the Greeks got there first. Hippocrates, Aristotle, Euclid, Galen, and Ptolemy wrote in Greek, and when the Renaissance rediscovered them, their vocabulary came along. Then, when the scientific revolution needed thousands of new words, Greek had the advantage of being a compounding language: you could stick two or three roots together and get something that sounded like a word. Latin could do it too, but Greek did it more naturally, and the habit stuck. By the nineteenth century, if you discovered something, you named it in Greek.

This chapter is a handful of the Greek roots that matter most, and the stories hiding in them. The fuller families, the medical kit, and the long tail of smaller roots are in the appendix.

## LOGOS: word, reason, study

*Logos* is the most important Greek root in English, and it means at least three things: word, reason, and account. The suffix *-logy* means "the study of," and it attaches to everything: *biology, geology, theology*, and any field you care to invent. *Etymology* is the study of the *etymon*, the true meaning of a word. *-logue* is a spoken thing: a *prologue* is the speech before, an *epilogue* the speech upon, after. A *eulogy* is good speech about someone, usually when they can no longer hear it. And *logistics* is probably not family at all; it is from French *loger*, to lodge, though Greek *logistikos*, skilled in reckoning, may have helped it along.

## GRAPH / GRAM: write, draw

*Graphein* meant to scratch, then to write, then to draw: *autograph, biography, geography*. *Photograph*, light-writing, was coined by John Herschel in 1839. A *paragraph* was originally a mark written beside the text to show a new section, not the section itself. A *program* is *pro* + *gramma*: something written up beforehand, a public notice.

*Grammar* has one of the best histories in the language. In the Middle Ages, *grammar* meant learning in general, particularly Latin, which to the illiterate looked indistinguishable from sorcery. So *gramarye* came to mean occult knowledge, and in Scots dialect the word was worn down to *glamour*, meaning a spell or enchantment. Walter Scott popularized it in the early nineteenth century, and by the twentieth century it meant the kind of enchantment Hollywood sells. *Glamour* and *grammar* are the same word, and both, in their way, are still about the power of knowing the right form.

## PATH: feeling, suffering

*Pathos* is suffering, experience, feeling: *sympathy* is feeling with, *apathy* is no feeling, *pathology* is the study of suffering, hence of disease. *Empathy*, feeling into, was coined in 1909 by the psychologist Edward Titchener to translate the German *Einfühlung*, so it is one of the newest words in this chapter. And *pathetic* originally just meant "affecting"; now it is a sneer.

## TOM: cut

*Temnein*, to cut, gives one of the most elegant clusters in English. An *atom* is *a* + *tomos*: uncuttable, the thing you reach when you can't divide any further. Democritus proposed the concept around 400 BC; it took about 2,300 years to find out he was wrong and it could be cut after all, and by then the name had stuck. *Anatomy* is cutting up. *Dichotomy* is cutting in two. Every *-ectomy* is a cutting out. An *epitome* was literally a cutting-upon, an abridgment, a summary; the modern sense of "the perfect example" came from the idea of a work that contains the whole in small. *Entomology* is the study of insects, from *entomon*, "cut-in," because insects have bodies cut into segments, and *insect* is the Latin translation of exactly that: *in* + *secare*, cut into. Two languages, one image, one bug.

## ERG / URG: work

*Ergon*, work, is the root of *energy* (Aristotle's *energeia*, being-at-work), *synergy* (working together), and *allergy* (*allos* + *ergon*: other-working; coined in 1906 by Clemens von Pirquet for a changed reaction). It is also, less obviously, *surgery*. That one is *cheir* (hand) + *ergon*: hand-work. A surgeon was originally a *chirurgeon*, and the word was worn down through French; a *chiropractor* is a hand-practitioner from the same *cheir*. The name *George* is *ge* + *ergon*, earth-worker, farmer. And *orgy*, from *orgia*, is probably the same root: secret rites, "works" performed for a god, which subsequently got a reputation.

## -CRACY and -ARCHY: rule

Greek had two words for power: *kratos*, strength, and *arche*, beginning or first place. *Democracy* is people-power; *aristocracy* is rule by the best; *kakistocracy*, a word that deserves more use, is rule by the worst. *Bureaucracy* is rule by desks, from French *bureau*. *Meritocracy* was first recorded in 1956 and made famous in 1958 by Michael Young, who used it as a term of abuse in a satirical novel; it is now used unironically, which would have annoyed him. On the *-archy* side, *hierarchy* was originally rule by priests (*hieros*, sacred); the modern sense of ranked layers comes from a fifth-century theologian who described the ranks of angels.

## The medical kit

Medicine is where Greek is most systematic, and it rewards learning as a set. A dozen suffixes (*-itis* inflammation, *-ectomy* removal, *-emia* blood condition, *-algia* pain) and a couple dozen body parts (*hepat-* liver, *nephr-* kidney, *oste-* bone, *rhin-* nose) combine freely. Once you have them, *hepatitis, nephrology, osteosarcoma,* and *rhinoplasty* are all words you can read without ever having seen them. The kit is in the appendix. One member deserves a mention here: *nostalgia* is *-algia* too, the pain of homecoming, and chapter twelve has its surprisingly clinical history.

## GEN: birth, kind

*Genesis, gene, generate.* To be *generous* was to be of noble birth, and so noble in behavior. A *genius* was a guardian spirit born with you. An *engine* is from *ingenium*, inborn talent, which is why an *engineer* and an *ingenious* person are close relatives. *Hydrogen* is water-producer and *oxygen* is acid-producer, and chapter ten explains why the second one was a mistake. Latin and Greek share this root, which is why *genus* (Latin) and *genesis* (Greek) sit side by side, and English *kin* and *kind* are the Germanic cousins.

## A few more worth knowing

A handful of the smaller roots have stories too good to leave in the appendix. *Cosm-* is order: the Greek verb *kosmein* meant both "to order" and "to adorn," so the *cosmos* is the universe considered as a beautifully arranged thing, and *cosmetic* is the same word. *Astr-* is star, and a *disaster* is a bad star. *The-* is god, and *enthusiasm* is having a god inside you. *Hydrophobia*, fear of water, was the old name for rabies. And a *horoscope* is an hour-watcher.

If the Latin chapters felt like learning the internals of the civil service, this one should feel like learning the internals of a hospital and a university, and that is roughly the division of labor the two languages settled on.
