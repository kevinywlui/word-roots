# 4. The Latin Verbs You Already Conjugate

Latin verbs are the workhorses of formal English. A couple dozen of them, each with a few spellings and each combining with the prefixes from the last chapter, generate several hundred everyday words. This chapter is a tour of the best stories in the most important of them. Read it for the connections; the full word families are in the appendix, for when you want to raid them.

## Make, take, carry

**FAC / FIC / FACT / FECT: make, do.** From *facere*, possibly the single most productive root in English. A *factory* is where things are made; a *fact* is a thing done. A *facsimile* is "make it similar," and *fax* is what happens when you say that quickly. *Satisfy* is *satis* (enough) + *facere*: to do enough. And *affect* and *effect* are the same root with different prefixes (to do something *to*, and what is made *out*), which is either a consolation or an insult to everyone who confuses them.

**CAP / CIP / CAPT / CEPT: take, seize.** From *capere*: *capture, accept, receive*. A *recipe* is the Latin imperative "take!", the first word of a prescription. My favorite is *emancipate*: *e* + *manus* (hand) + *capere*, to take out of someone's hand, from a Roman legal ceremony for releasing a son from his father's power.

**PORT: carry.** From *portare*: *portable, export, transport*. *Opportunity* is *ob* + *portus*, harbor: a wind carrying you toward port, a favorable moment. And *sport* is *disport*, to carry oneself away from work.

**FER / LAT: carry, bear.** From *ferre*, and here is a wonderful irregularity. Latin *ferre* borrowed its past participle from a completely different verb, so the past participle of "carry" was *latum*. English inherited both halves without telling anyone they belonged together. So *refer* and *relate* are the same verb: carry back. *Transfer* and *translate*: carry across (a translation carries meaning across languages; a bishop *translated* to a new see has been carried across). *Confer* and *collate*: carry together. And a *legislator* is *legis* (of the law) + *lator* (bearer): one who carries the law.

## See, speak, send

**SPEC / SPECT: look.** From *specere*: *inspect, respect, spectator*. *Auspicious* is from *avis* (bird) + *specere*: watching birds for omens, an *auspex* being the Roman official who did so. To *speculate* was to watch from a lookout tower, a *specula*, and only later to theorize or to gamble on the market. And *species* was just the look of a thing, its kind, until Linnaeus made it technical.

**VID / VIS: see.** From *videre*. *Video* is simply Latin for "I see." To *provide* is to see ahead, and *providence*, contracted, became *prudence*: foresight. *Improvise* is the reverse, the unforeseen, done on the spot. And *envy* is *invidere*, to look at with hostility, the evil eye; *invidious* still has the sting.

**DIC / DICT: say.** From *dicere*: *dictionary, predict, contradict*. A *verdict* is a true saying, from *verus*. An *addict* was originally a person given over to someone by a court's decree, long before it meant a person given over to a habit.

**MIT / MISS: send.** From *mittere*: *mission, missile, transmit, dismiss*. A *promise* is something sent forward, a word sent ahead of the deed, and a *compromise* is a promise made mutually.

## Stand, hold, bind

**STA / STAT / SIST: stand.** This root has the distinction of being the same root in Latin, Greek, and English; *stand*, *stasis*, and *status* are cousins going back six thousand years to a Proto-Indo-European verb. A *solstice* is the day the sun stands still; an *armistice* is a standing-still of arms. *Ecstasy* is the Greek version, *ek* + *stasis*: standing outside yourself. *Superstition* is standing over something, though nobody is certain what the original image was; the best guess is standing over it in awe. And a *stable* is where the horses stand.

**TEN / TENT / TAIN: hold.** From *tenere*: *tenant, contain, retain*. A *tenet* is Latin for "he holds." A *continent* is land that holds together. A *lieutenant* is one holding a place (*lieu*) for a superior; the British pronounce it "leftenant" for reasons nobody has satisfactorily explained.

**STRING / STRICT: draw tight, bind.** From *stringere*: *strict, restrict, stress*. *Prestige* is the surprise: from *praestigiae*, a conjurer's tricks, from *praestringere*, to bind fast or blindfold. A person of prestige was originally one who dazzled by illusion. The word meant "deception" until the nineteenth century, when it turned into a compliment.

## Go and come

**CED / CESS: go, yield.** From *cedere*: *proceed, recede, exceed*. An *ancestor* is from *antecessor*, one who went before. And *necessary* is probably *ne* + *cedere*: what does not yield, what cannot be got out of the way.

**VEN / VENT: come.** From *venire*: *convene, event, venue*. To *invent* was to come upon something, to find it. To *prevent* was to come before, to anticipate; it only later came to mean to stop. And a *souvenir* is what comes back to mind.

That is thirteen verbs and a few stories each. Add *duc* (lead), *pon* (put), *jac* (throw), *pel* (drive), *voc* (call), and a few others from the appendix, and you can decompose a large fraction of the abstract vocabulary of English on sight.
