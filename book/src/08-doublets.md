# 8. Words That Came Twice

The French layer and the direct-from-Latin layer overlap. Many Latin words entered English through Old French in the Middle Ages, wearing French sound changes, and then entered again in the Renaissance straight from the Latin dictionary. The two arrivals settled in as separate words, usually with the French one meaning something everyday and the Latin one meaning something formal. Linguists call these doublets, and they are the best evidence that sound change is lawful rather than random.

The last two came through Old English rather than French, but the wearing-down is the same. Some of these deserve a sentence.

**Treason and tradition** are both *traditio*, a handing-over. You can hand over a body of custom to the next generation, or hand over a city to the enemy. A *traitor* is a *traditor*, one who hands over. Early Christians used *traditor* for those who handed the scriptures to Roman persecutors, and the word never recovered.

**Poison and potion** are both *potio*, a drink. The difference is what's in it. *Potable* is from the same verb.

**Spice and species.** Latin *species* meant appearance, then kind or sort, and in Late Latin, "goods," specifically the sorted merchandise a trader dealt in. Via Old French *espice*, this became *spice*: the goods par excellence, the ones worth sailing around Africa for.

**Story and history** are both Greek *historia* (inquiry) through Latin. *History* kept the sense of inquiry into the past; *story* drifted toward narrative of any kind. The historian and the storyteller are the same word, which is a fact both professions might prefer to keep quiet.

**Blame and blaspheme.** *Blasphemein*, to speak evil of, entered Old French as *blasmer* and came out as *blame*. To blame someone is, etymologically, to blaspheme them. The direct borrowing kept its theological register.

**Dainty and dignity.** *Dignitas*, worth. A dainty was originally a thing of worth, a delicacy; the adjective followed. Both words mean "worthy," but one is a duchess and the other is a petit four.

**Chance and cadence.** *Cadere*, to fall. A chance is how the dice fall; a cadence is how the voice falls at the end of a phrase. *Accident* (a falling-toward), *decadence* (a falling away), *coincide*, *deciduous* (falling off), *case* (how things fall out, and the grammatical case; the case that holds things is a different word), *casual, cascade, occasion* (a falling-toward, an opportunity), and *cadaver* (the fallen) are all the same fall.

## Why the French forms look the way they do

The sound changes are consistent enough that you can learn to see through them. Three rules cover most of the territory.

**Latin *ca-* becomes French *cha-*.** Chapter five showed this with *cattle* and *chattel*; here is the general rule. *Caput* to *chief*, *castellum* to *chateau*, *cantare* to *chant*, *carus* to *cher* (and *cherish*), *capitulum* to *chapter*, *cattus* to *chat*. Norman French, the dialect that actually invaded England, did not make this change, which is why English sometimes has both: *cattle* and *chattel*, *catch* and *chase* (both from *captiare*, to seize), and *castle* and *chateau*.

**Latin *-tion* stays; Latin *-atus* becomes *-é* or *-ee*.** *Nation, station, ration* look Latin because French kept them nearly intact. But the past participle *-atus* became *-é*, and English took it as *-ee* in *employee, referee, trustee, refugee*: one to whom the action is done. The doer got *-er* or *-or*. So *employer* and *employee* encode a Latin voice distinction through a French filter, which is more grammar than most people know they're using.

**Latin *p* between vowels becomes French *v*.** *Sapere* (to taste, to be wise) gives French *savoir*, and its noun *sapor* gives English *savor*; the direct Latin borrowing gives *sapient* and *Homo sapiens*. *Ripa* (riverbank) gives *river* through French; the Latin form gives *riparian*. *Capra* (goat) gives *chevron* through French (originally a rafter; the Romans called rafters little goats) and *Capricorn* directly.

You do not need to memorize these. But once you have seen a few, you start to recognize the two costumes, and to guess that a plain-sounding English word with a soft *ch* or a missing consonant has a starchier Latin twin somewhere in the dictionary. Usually it does.
