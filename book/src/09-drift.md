# 9. Semantic Drift, or Why Nice Meant Stupid

Knowing a word's parts tells you what it meant when it was assembled. It does not tell you what it means now, because meanings drift, and they drift in predictable directions. This chapter is about the drift, partly because it is fun and partly because it is the thing that makes root-based guessing fail, and you should know when to expect failure.

## The direction of drift

There are a few standard patterns.

**Narrowing.** *Meat* once meant food in general and *deer* once meant any animal; both are Germanic, but the pattern is universal. Latin *species* narrowed from "kind" to a biological rank, and *doctor* from "teacher" to "physician."

**Broadening.** *Decimate* broadened from "kill one in ten" to "devastate." *Virtue* broadened from *virtus*, manliness, courage, to goodness in general, and then narrowed again to chastity in particular contexts.

**Pejoration** (getting worse). This is common, and it happens to words for women, servants, and peasants with depressing regularity. *Villain* is from *villanus*, a farm worker at a *villa*. *Silly* (Germanic) went from blessed to innocent to pitiable to foolish. *Pathetic* went from "moving" to "contemptible." *Notorious* went from "well known" to "well known for something bad." *Egregious* went from *e grege*, standing out from the flock, exceptional, to exceptionally bad. *Sinister* is just Latin for "left," and the superstition that the left hand is unlucky did the rest.

**Amelioration** (getting better). Rarer. *Nice* is the classic. From *nescius*, ignorant (*ne* + *scire*, not knowing; the same root as *science*), it meant foolish in the thirteenth century, then fussy, then precise (a "nice distinction" still means a fine one), then pleasant. It took some five hundred years to travel from an insult to a compliment, and it spent most of that time meaning something like "overly particular." *Prestige* went from trickery to renown (chapter four). *Shrewd* went from wicked to sharp. *Terrific* went from terrifying to great, and *awesome* is doing the same thing right now.

**Concrete to abstract.** This is the big one, and it is the source of most of the surprises in etymology. Abstract words are almost always dead metaphors: a concrete image that got used figuratively until nobody remembered the image. To *understand* was (probably) to stand among. To *comprehend* was to grasp. To *consider* was (probably) to look at the stars. *Abstract* itself is *abs* + *tractus*: dragged away.

## A short tour of dead metaphors

Some of these are so good they justify the whole enterprise.

**Muscle** is *musculus*, little mouse, because a flexing biceps looks like a mouse moving under the skin. The Greeks had the same idea: *mys* meant both mouse and muscle, and gives us *myalgia* and *myocardium*.

**Pupil** (of the eye) is *pupilla*, little doll, because you see a tiny reflected figure of yourself in someone's eye. **Pupil** (the student) is the same word in the sense of a little one, a ward. Both come from *pupa*, a girl or doll, which is also the name for the stage of an insect's life when it looks like a swaddled infant.

**Calculus** is a pebble. Romans counted with pebbles on a board, so *calculare* meant to reckon. A kidney stone is also a calculus, and that sense is older than the mathematics. Newton and Leibniz's calculus is "the pebble-reckoning," a name it got from the phrase *calculus differentialis*.

**Salary** is from *salarium*, which is from *sal*, salt. The popular story is that Roman soldiers were paid in salt. The evidence for this is weak; the more likely account is that the *salarium* was an allowance for buying salt, or a payment named after salt because salt was the paradigm of a necessary purchase. Either way, the salt is real, and "worth his salt" is a translation of the etymology.

**Trivial** is *trivium*, a place where three roads meet. What you find at a crossroads is common, public, ordinary gossip, hence *trivialis*, commonplace. Separately, the medieval *trivium* was the first three liberal arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), the introductory course before the *quadrivium*; that sense reinforced the "elementary" flavor.

**Companion** is *com* + *panis*, with-bread: someone you share bread with. *Company* is the same. The Germanic word *lord* is *hlaf-weard*, loaf-guardian, and *lady* is *hlaf-dige*, loaf-kneader. Bread turns out to be the foundation of social structure in both languages.

**Sarcasm** is from *sarkazein*, to tear flesh like a dog, from *sarx*, flesh. A *sarcophagus* is a flesh-eater: the Greeks believed a certain limestone dissolved bodies placed in it, and used it for coffins. *Sarcoma* is a tumor of the flesh.

**Candidate** we covered: a man in a whitened toga. **Ambition** is *ambire*, to go around, specifically to go around canvassing for votes. Roman politics gave us both words, and *ambition*, at least, was not originally a compliment.

**Curfew** is French *couvre-feu*, cover-fire: the evening bell that told you to bank your hearth. **Garble** is from Arabic via Italian, to sift spices, and meant to sort until it meant to mix up.

## The astrology layer

Which brings up a whole stratum of English that most people walk past. Medieval and Renaissance Europe took astrology seriously, and the vocabulary of astrology settled into ordinary English as the belief evaporated.

*Influence* is *influentia*, an in-flowing: the ethereal fluid that streamed from the stars and shaped your character. When Chaucer used it, he meant the astral kind. *Consider* and *desire* may be sidereal, as chapter five mentioned. *Disaster* is *dis* + *astrum*, a bad star, via Italian *disastro*. *Lunatic* is one afflicted by the moon. *Jovial* is born under Jupiter (Jove), and so cheerful; *saturnine* under Saturn, and so gloomy; *mercurial* under Mercury, and so changeable; *martial* under Mars, and so warlike; *venereal* under Venus, and so, well. A *sanguine* temperament, a *melancholy* one (Greek: black bile), a *phlegmatic* one, a *choleric* one: the four humors, another abandoned theory that left its whole vocabulary behind.

This is worth noticing as a general pattern: a theory dies, and its words keep walking around. We still say the sun *rises*. We still call it *hysteria* (from *hystera*, womb; the theory was that it was a disease of the uterus). We say someone is in *good humor*. *Cretin* is from a Swiss French dialect word for Christian, applied to people with congenital thyroid disease as a reminder that they were human. The fossil record of English includes the fossils of ideas.

## The mythology layer

The same for the gods. *Panic* is from Pan, whose shout terrified armies. *Tantalize* is from Tantalus, condemned to stand in water he could not drink. *Atlas*, the book, is from Atlas holding up the sky, because Mercator put him on the title page. *Hypnosis* is Hypnos, sleep. *Morphine* is Morpheus, dreams. *Nemesis* was the goddess of retribution. *Cereal* is Ceres. *Volcano* is Vulcan. *Museum* is a temple of the Muses, and *music* is their art. *Hermetic* is from Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary alchemist, and means sealed because he was believed to have invented a way of sealing vessels. *Fortune, flora, fauna, cupidity, erotic, narcissism, echo, mentor, chaos, titanic, protean, herculean, odyssey, siren, nectar, ambrosia.* The Greeks and Romans stopped worshipping their gods but never stopped using their names, and neither have we.

The lesson for the reader of unknown words is this: decomposition gives you the original picture, and the original picture is usually a good hint. But the current meaning is wherever the drift stopped, and when the parts and the word disagree, the word wins.
