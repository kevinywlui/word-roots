# 10. The Neologism Factory

Most of the Latin and Greek in English arrived organically, through conquest and scholarship. But a large and growing share was manufactured: coined on purpose, by identifiable people, on identifiable dates, out of classical parts. Science is the main factory, and the way scientists name things tells you a lot about both the science and the scientists.

## Chemistry: named for what they thought it did

The chemical elements are a museum of eighteenth-century assumptions. Antoine Lavoisier, who more or less invented modern chemistry in the 1780s, named two gases. *Hydrogen* is *hydro* + *gen*, water-producer, because burning it makes water. Correct. *Oxygen* is *oxy* (sharp, acid) + *gen*, acid-producer, because Lavoisier believed all acids contained oxygen. Wrong: hydrochloric acid has none. The name outlived the theory by two centuries and counting. The German word *Sauerstoff*, "sour stuff," is the same mistake translated.

The noble gases were named in a burst in the 1890s by William Ramsay, and he named them for the experience of discovering them. *Argon* is *argos*, lazy, because it would not react with anything. *Neon* is *neos*, new. *Krypton* is *kryptos*, hidden. *Xenon* is *xenos*, stranger. *Helium* had been named earlier, in 1868, from *helios*, sun, because it was first detected in the solar spectrum during an eclipse, before anyone had found it on Earth. The *-ium* ending is the Latin ending for metals (*sodium, potassium, calcium*), and the astronomers who named it assumed it was one. It is not, and it is the only noble gas with a metal's name.

*Technetium* is *technetos*, artificial: the first element made rather than found. *Promethium* was named for the Titan who stole fire, because it came out of a nuclear reactor. The elements named after places and people (*polonium, einsteinium*) are Latinized, not Latin, but the *-ium* still marks them as members of the club.

Then there are the suffixes. Sugars end in *-ose* (*sucrose, lactose, fructose*), by analogy with *glucose*, which was from Greek *gleukos*, sweet wine. Enzymes end in *-ase* (*lactase, protease*), which is nothing but the *-ase* of *diastase*, the first enzyme named, chopped off and reused. Alcohols end in *-ol*. Alkaloids end in *-ine*. None of this was designed; each was a single coinage that became a template.

## Biology: named by a committee that spoke Greek

*Protein* was coined in 1838 by the Dutch chemist Gerardus Mulder, on a suggestion from Berzelius, from Greek *proteios*, "of the first rank," because he believed it was the fundamental substance of all living matter. He was more right than Lavoisier.

*Dinosaur* was coined in 1842 by Richard Owen from *deinos* (terrible) + *sauros* (lizard). Dinosaurs are not lizards. *Pterodactyl* is wing-finger. *Tyrannosaurus rex* is tyrant-lizard king, half Greek, half Latin. *Triceratops* is three-horn-face. Paleontologists have been naming things in Greek ever since, partly because Greek compounds sound impressive and partly because the international rules of nomenclature require names to be Latinized, and Greek Latinizes easily.

*Photosynthesis* is light-putting-together. *Chlorophyll* is green-leaf. *Mitochondrion* is thread-granule. *Chromosome* is colored body. The whole of cell biology is named after what things looked like under a nineteenth-century microscope, in the language of a civilization that had never seen a cell.

## Physics: named for amber and going

*Electricity* comes from *elektron*, Greek for amber, because rubbing amber was the ancient world's only known way of producing static. William Gilbert coined *electricus* in 1600. The *electron* was named in 1891 by George Johnstone Stoney, before it was discovered, as the name for the fundamental unit of charge.

*Ion* is Greek for "going," and was suggested to Michael Faraday in the 1830s by William Whewell, a philosopher who was very good at this sort of thing. Whewell also gave us *anode* (way up), *cathode* (way down), *anion, cation*, and the word *scientist* itself, in 1833, by analogy with *artist*. Before Whewell, scientists were "natural philosophers."

*Energy* is Aristotle's *energeia*, repurposed by Thomas Young in 1807. *Entropy* was coined by Rudolf Clausius in 1865 from *en* + *trope* (turning), deliberately made to resemble *energy*. *Photon* is from *phos*, light, with the *-on* of *electron*. *Proton* is "first." *Neutron* is Latin *neuter* with a Greek suffix. *Quark* is from *Finnegans Wake* and has no classical roots at all; Murray Gell-Mann chose it in 1964 as a deliberate break from the tradition that had already produced *hadron* (thick), *lepton* (thin), and *baryon* (heavy). The tradition mostly ignored him.

## Computing: named for pruning and steering

*Computer* is Latin *computare*, *com* + *putare*. *Putare* meant to prune, to clean, and from there to reckon up and to think. (*Reputation* is what people reckon you to be; *dispute* is to reckon apart; *deputy* is one assigned by reckoning.) Until the 1940s a computer was a person, usually a woman, who did calculations.

*Data* is the Latin plural of *datum*, "a thing given." *Digital* is from *digitus*, finger, because you count on them. *Calculator* is from the pebbles. *Algorithm* is not classical at all: it is the name of the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi, Latinized as *algorismus*, and then misspelled under the influence of Greek *arithmos*, number. *Cybernetics* was coined in 1948 by Norbert Wiener from *kybernetes*, steersman. Latin had borrowed that Greek word as *gubernator*, which became *governor*. So *cyber* and *govern* are the same word, which is a little too apt.

## The hybrid problem, and its resolution

For two centuries there was a rule, never enforced but widely felt, that a proper coinage should be all Greek or all Latin. *Television, automobile, sociology, homosexual* (Greek *homo*, same, plus Latin *sexus*; coined in 1869 by Karl-Maria Kertbeny), *bureaucracy, meritocracy* all broke it and, as chapter two mentioned, were sneered at. They all survived. The lesson from the neologism factory is that etymological purity has never once determined whether a word lives; sound and need determine that. The purists were right that the words were mongrels and wrong that it mattered.
