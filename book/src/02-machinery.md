# 2. How Words Are Assembled

A classical word in English is a sandwich: an optional prefix, a root, and an optional suffix. Sometimes two roots. Occasionally two prefixes. That is nearly the whole grammar, and if you internalize it you will start seeing seams in words you have used your whole life.

Take *incomprehensible*. It looks like a single sixteen-letter object. It is actually four parts:

| in- | com- | prehend | -ible |
|---|---|---|---|
| not | together | grasp | able to be |

"Not able to be grasped together." *Prehend* is the same root as in *prehensile* (a monkey's tail that grasps), *apprehend* (grasp toward, hence arrest), and *comprehend* (grasp fully). The suffix *-ible* is the same as *-able*; Latin had two spellings depending on the verb class, and English inherited both, which is why you have to memorize that it is *credible* but *dependable*. There is no rule. Sorry.

Once you start looking, the seams are everywhere. *Disruptive* is *dis-* (apart) + *rupt* (break) + *-ive* (tending to). *Rupt* is also in *erupt, interrupt, corrupt, bankrupt* (a broken bench, from the Italian moneylender's *banca rotta*), and *rupture*. *Transparent* is *trans-* (through) + *par* (appear) + *-ent* (doing). *Circumstance* is *circum-* (around) + *sta* (stand) + *-ance*: the things standing around you.

## The parts slide into each other

Latin did something that makes root-spotting harder: when a prefix ended in a consonant and the root began with one, the prefix consonant frequently changed to match. This is called assimilation, and it is why the same prefix wears so many costumes.

The prefix *ad-* (to, toward) is the champion. It appears as:

- *ad-* in *admit, advocate, adhere*
- *ac-* in *accept, accord, acquire*
- *af-* in *affect, affirm, affluent*
- *ag-* in *aggregate, aggressive*
- *al-* in *allocate, allude*
- *an-* in *announce, annex*
- *ap-* in *appear, apply, approve*
- *ar-* in *arrive, arrogant*
- *as-* in *assimilate, assume, assign*
- *at-* in *attract, attend, attempt*

The doubled consonant is the fingerprint. When you see an English word beginning with a vowel and then a doubled consonant, there is a good chance a prefix has melted into a root. *Accept* is *ad* + *capere* (take): take toward oneself. *Attract* is *ad* + *trahere* (drag). *Announce* is *ad* + *nuntiare* (report; a *nuncio* is a papal messenger).

The same thing happens with:

- *in-* (not, or in): *illegal, immobile, irregular, imbibe, illuminate*
- *con-* (with, together): *collect, combine, correct, cooperate* (and plain *co-* before vowels)
- *sub-* (under): *succeed, suffer, suggest, support, suspend* (with an extra *s*: *sus-*)
- *ob-* (against, toward): *occur, offer, oppose*
- *ex-* (out): *effect, efface,* and plain *e-* before many consonants, as in *emit, evade, elect*
- *dis-* (apart): *differ, diffuse,* and *di-* in *divert, digest*

Greek did it too. *Syn-* (together) becomes *syl-* in *syllable*, *sym-* in *symmetry* and *symphony*, and *sy-* in *system*. *En-* (in) becomes *em-* in *embryo* and *emphasis*.

The practical upshot: when a word begins with a short vowel followed by a double consonant, try peeling off a prefix. It works surprisingly often.

## The root itself changes shape

Latin verbs came in four "principal parts," and English borrowed from at least two of them, which is why one root can look like two. The verb *facere* (to make, do) has the stem *fac-* in *facile* and *facsimile*, but its past participle *factus* gives *fact* and *manufacture*, and when it followed a prefix the vowel weakened, giving *-fic-* in *efficient, deficit, magnificent* and *-fect-* in *perfect, infect, defect, effect, affect*. That is one verb, four spellings. Similarly:

- *capere* (take): *cap-, -cip-, capt-, -cept-*. *Capable, anticipate, captive, concept.*
- *tenere* (hold): *ten-, -tin-, tent-*. *Tenant, continent, content* (what is held in).
- *cadere* (fall): *cad-, -cid-, cas-*. *Cadence, accident, case.*
- *mittere* (send): *mitt-, miss-*. *Transmit, mission.*
- *scribere* (write): *scrib-, script-*. *Describe, description.*
- *videre* (see): *vid-, vis-*. *Evident, vision.*

You do not need to learn the rules of Latin vowel weakening. You need to know that it happened, so that when you see *-cip-* in *recipient* you think "probably *cap*, take," and you are right.

## Telling Greek from Latin

Why would you want to? Two reasons. First, Greek and Latin roots tend to combine with their own kind, so knowing which family a word belongs to narrows your guess. Second, Greek words cluster in science and medicine and Latin in law and administration, and the register tells you something.

The spelling tells are reliable:

- **ph, th, ch, rh, and y in the middle of a word are Greek.** *Philosophy, theater, chorus, rhythm, hypothesis, physics.* Latin had none of these letters; they were invented to transliterate Greek. (A *y* at the end of a word, as in *happy* or *reality*, means nothing; it is just an English spelling.)
- **Words ending in *-sis*, *-ma*, *-on*, or *-ic* skew Greek.** *Analysis, drama, phenomenon, cosmic.*
- **Words ending in *-tion*, *-ity*, *-ment*, *-ance*, or *-ous* skew Latin.** *Nation, quality, argument, tolerance, famous.*
- **The joining vowel.** When two Greek roots are glued together, the glue is usually *-o-*: *therm-o-meter, phil-o-sophy, psych-o-logy.* When two Latin roots are glued, it is usually *-i-*: *agr-i-culture, omn-i-potent, carn-i-vore.* This is one of the best tells there is.

That last rule also flags the hybrids: words with one Greek part and one Latin part, which purists once considered a kind of miscegenation. *Television* is Greek *tele* (far) plus Latin *visio* (seeing). C. P. Scott, editor of the *Manchester Guardian*, is reported to have said: "Television? The word is half Greek and half Latin. No good will come of it." *Automobile* is Greek *auto* plus Latin *mobilis*. *Sociology* is Latin *socius* plus Greek *logos*; when Auguste Comte coined it in the 1830s, critics sneered at the mongrel; the all-Greek alternative would have had to be built on *koinonia*, and nobody has ever wanted to say *koinonology*. Hybrids won. The purists lost. The language does not care.

## A note on what a "root" is

I am going to use the word "root" loosely, to mean any recurring chunk of a word that carries meaning. Linguists distinguish roots (the bare core) from stems (root plus some grammatical material) from combining forms (the version that shows up in compounds). For reading English, the distinctions rarely matter. What matters is being able to look at *photosynthesis* and see *phot* (light), *syn* (together), *the* (put; a different *the-* from the one in *theology*), *-sis* (process): the process of putting things together with light. Which is exactly what it is.
