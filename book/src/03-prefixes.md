# 3. Prefixes: The Forty-Odd Words That Do All the Work

Prefixes are the highest-value items in the kit. There are only a few dozen, they are used constantly, and most of them mean something spatial, which makes them easy to picture: *in* and *out*, *over* and *under*, *before* and *after*, *through*, *around*, *against*. Most come in matched pairs, one Latin and one Greek: *super-* and *hyper-* both mean over, *circum-* and *peri-* both mean around, so a *circumference* and a *perimeter* are the same idea in two languages. The full set, grouped by meaning, is in the appendix. This chapter is about the ones with stories in them.

## Position and direction

**Ob-** means "in the way of," and it is hiding in some good words. *Obvious* is *ob* + *via*: in the road, right in front of you. *Obstacle* is *ob* + *stare*: something standing in the way. *Occur* is *ob* + *currere*: to run into.

**Per-** means "through" but also "thoroughly," and the second sense is worth knowing. *Perfect* is *per* + *factus*: thoroughly made, completely done. *Perjury* is to swear through and through, in the bad sense of "all the way to falsehood."

**Para-** in Greek meant "beside," and it drifted into "beside the normal," hence "faulty" or "beyond." A *paradox* is *para* + *doxa*: beside received opinion. A *parasite* is *para* + *sitos* (food): one who eats beside you, at your table, a professional dinner guest, which is what the word originally meant in Greek before biology got hold of it.

**Apo-** means "away from," and gives a nice cluster of religious words. An *apostle* is one sent away. An *apocalypse* is an uncovering, *apo* + *kalyptein* (to cover), which is why the last book of the New Testament is also called Revelation; they are the same word in two languages. *Apology*, from *apo* + *logos*, was originally a speech made in one's own defense; Plato's *Apology* is Socrates defending himself, not saying sorry.

**Epi-** means "upon." An *epidemic* is a disease upon the people (*demos*); a *pandemic* is upon all the people; an *endemic* disease is one that lives in the people. The *epicenter* of an earthquake is the point on the surface directly upon the actual center, which is underground and called the *hypocenter*. When journalists use "epicenter" to mean "the center," they are technically pointing at the wrong spot.

**Meta-** deserves its own paragraph because it has become so fashionable. In Greek it meant "after" or "beyond" or "change." *Metamorphosis* is a change of form. The modern sense of "about itself, one level up" comes from an accident of library organization: when Aristotle's works were arranged in the first century BC, the untitled books that came after the *Physics* were labeled *ta meta ta physika*, "the ones after the physics." Those books happened to be about first principles and the nature of being. So *metaphysics* came to mean "the study of what lies beyond the physical," and *meta-* came to mean "above and about," and now we have metadata and metacognition and people saying "that's so meta." All from a shelving decision.

## Quantity and quality

**The two *in-* prefixes.** Latin had two prefixes that both came out as *in-*. One means "not" (*invisible*). The other means "in, into," and often just intensifies (*inflame*). Usually context sorts it out. Sometimes it doesn't. *Inflammable* uses the second one: it means "able to be set aflame," the same as *flammable*. Enough people read it as "not flammable" that the word *flammable* was actively promoted by fire-safety organizations in the twentieth century to replace it on warning labels. *Invaluable* uses the first: "not able to be valued," so precious it is beyond pricing. *Inhabitable* uses the second again: "able to be lived in," not "un-habitable." If a word beginning with *in-* seems to contradict itself, one of the two prefixes is usually the culprit.

**Greek *a-* (or *an-* before a vowel)** is the "alpha privative," and it is one of the most productive negatives in scientific English. *Atom* (uncuttable), *anonymous* (no name), *anesthesia* (no sensation), *atrophy* (no nourishment). When you see an *a-* on the front of a Greek-looking word, try "without."

**Dys-** versus **dis-.** They sound identical, and they are unrelated. Greek *dys-* means bad or difficult: *dyslexia* (bad reading), *dystopia* (bad place). Latin *dis-* means apart or not: *dismiss, disconnect*.

*Dystopia* has a good origin. Thomas More coined *Utopia* in 1516 from Greek *ou* (not) + *topos* (place): no-place, nowhere. But it was also a pun on *eu-topos*, good place, and the pun is why the word came to mean "ideal society." When John Stuart Mill wanted an opposite in 1868, he reached for *dys-* and said *dystopia* in Parliament. He was not quite the first (a stray *dustopia* turns up in 1747), but he is the reason the word stuck, and in using it he quietly confirmed that everyone had been hearing the pun all along.

**Hyper-** and **hypo-** are the ones most likely to matter in a hospital. *Hyper* is over, *hypo* is under. *Hyperthermia* is too hot, *hypothermia* is too cold. *Hypoglycemia* is too little sugar in the blood. *Hypothesis* is something placed under, a foundation you build an argument on. The only trick is remembering which is which, and the mnemonic I use is that *hyper* has the higher-sounding vowel. It is not a good mnemonic. It works anyway.
