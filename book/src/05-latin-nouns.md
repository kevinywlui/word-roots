# 5. Heads, Hands, Hearts, and Feet

Latin nouns entered English mostly as the second half of compounds or as the base of adjectives. The body parts are the best place to start, because they are concrete, they are everywhere, and they show off the strangest feature of the whole system: how a single Latin word can arrive in English three or four separate times with a different face each time.

## CAPUT: head

*Caput* (stem *capit-*) meant head. Directly from Latin we get *capital* (the head city; the head sum of money on which interest accrues), *decapitate*, and *per capita* (by heads). To *capitulate* was to draw up terms under headings, and so to surrender. A *chapter* is a *capitulum*, a little head. So, oddly, is a *cabbage*, via French *caboche*, "big head."

Now watch what French does. Latin *ca-* at the start of a word turned into *cha-* in Parisian French but stayed *ca-* in the Norman dialect. So *caput* came into English as *chief* (Parisian: the head man) and later again as *chef* (the head of the kitchen, re-borrowed in the nineteenth century with its French pronunciation intact). *Capital* came through Norman French as *cattle* and through Parisian French as *chattel*, and both meant "movable property." In a medieval economy where wealth was livestock, "capital" and "cattle" were the same thing, and the language remembers it. *Captain* is a head man; a *cape* is a headland. That is one Latin noun, at least ten English words, and three different pronunciations of the first syllable.

## MANUS: hand

*Manual, manufacture, manuscript*: all done by hand. To *manipulate* is to handle, from *manipulus*, a handful, which was also the word for a Roman army unit small enough to be handled. And *manure* is the same word as *maneuver*, both from *manu operare*, to work by hand; a farmer manured a field by working it, and the stuff he spread on it took the name.

## PES / PED: foot

*Pedal, pedestrian, centipede.* To *impede* is to put the feet in fetters; to *expedite* is to free them, and so to speed things up. A *pedigree* is French *pied de grue*, crane's foot, from the branching shape of a genealogical diagram. The *pawn* in chess is the foot soldier, and a *pioneer* was a foot soldier sent ahead to dig.

Not to be confused with Greek *paid-* (child), spelled *ped-* or *paed-* in English, in *pediatrics* and *pedagogy*. Those are unrelated, and a pediatrician is not a foot doctor.

## COR and CORPUS: heart and body

*Cordial* is from the heart, and *courage* is *cor* through French. To *record* is *re* + *cor*: to call back to the heart, to learn by heart, which is what recording was before machines. My favorite is *misericord*, a heart of mercy, which is also the name of the little ledge on a choir stall that lets a monk rest while appearing to stand.

*Corpus* gives *corpse*, *corps* (a body of troops), and *corporation* (a body of people treated as a legal person). *Habeas corpus* is "you shall have the body," the writ demanding a prisoner be produced. But a military *corporal* is not bodily at all; that one is from *caput* via Italian *caporale*, a head man, and got bent toward *corpus* by resemblance.

## ANIMA and MENS: soul and mind

*Anima* is breath and soul, hence *animal*: a breathing thing. The soul words are the good ones: *unanimous* (of one mind), *magnanimous* (great-souled), and its glorious opposite *pusillanimous*, tiny-souled. *Mens* is mind: *mental*, *dementia* (mind gone away), and *memento*, the Latin imperative "remember!" The Greek counterpart, *psyche*, gave us *psychedelic*, mind-manifesting, coined in 1956 by the psychiatrist Humphry Osmond in a letter to Aldous Huxley.

## LUX and CANDERE: light and white

*Lux* gives *lucid* and *Lucifer*, the light-bearer, originally the morning star, which is Venus. And *candere*, to glow white, gives *candle*, *incandescent*, *candid* (white, pure, hence frank), and *candidate*: a Roman seeking office wore a specially whitened toga, a *toga candida*, so he was a *candidatus*, a whitened man. The gap between the etymology and the modern politician is left as an exercise.

## Earth, water, fire, and sky

The elements are mostly straightforward (*terrain, aquarium, ignite, marine, solar*), with a few treats. The *Mediterranean* is the sea in the middle of the lands. A *terrier* is a dog that goes into the earth after its prey. A *lunatic* is moonstruck. And from *sidus* (stem *sider-*, star), *consider* may once have meant to examine the stars, an augural term, and *desire* may be *de sidere*, "from the stars," to await what the stars will bring. Both are pretty and both are disputed.

The Greek parallels are *geo-* (earth), *hydro-* (water), *pyro-* (fire), *helio-* (sun), and *astro-* (star), and chapter seven is about why Greek got the science and Latin got the law.
