# 6. Counting in Two Dead Languages

Numbers are the tidiest part of the kit, and they also contain the most famous scandal in the English calendar. Latin and Greek each had a full set, and English uses both, often side by side: a *monopoly* and a *unicorn*, a *pentagon* and a *quintet*, a *kilogram* and a *millennium*. The counting is easy and the table is in the appendix. The stories are better.

**The calendar is off by two.** *September, October, November, December* are the seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth months by their names, and the ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth by the calendar. The reason is that the early Roman year began in March; January and February were added at the end and later moved to the front. The names never got fixed. (*July* and *August* were *Quintilis* and *Sextilis*, fifth and sixth, until they were renamed for Julius and Augustus Caesar. Nobody dared rename the rest.)

**A mile is a thousand.** The word is *mille passus*, a thousand paces, and a Roman *passus* was a double step, left foot to left foot, about five feet. A Roman mile was therefore about five thousand feet, and the modern mile of 5,280 feet is a sixteenth-century English compromise with the furlong.

**Decimate means one in ten.** The Roman army punished a mutinous unit by executing one soldier in ten, chosen by lot. So *decimate* originally meant to reduce by a tenth. Today it means to destroy most of something, and there is a certain kind of person who will correct you about this. We will discuss whether that person is right in chapter eleven. (Short answer: no.)

**Dilemma is Greek, not Latin.** *Di* + *lemma*, two premises: an argument that traps you between two conclusions. The horns of a dilemma were a later, extremely good metaphor.

**Quarantine is forty.** From Italian *quaranta giorni*, forty days, the period Venice held ships arriving from plague ports, from the fifteenth century; Latin *quadraginta* underneath. The number has since become negotiable.

**Sesquipedalian.** *Sesqui* (one and a half) + *ped* (foot): a foot-and-a-half long. Horace used *sesquipedalia verba*, "foot-and-a-half-long words," as a joke about pompous poets, and English borrowed the joke intact. It is the only self-describing word in this book.

**Octopus.** It is Greek: *okto* + *pous*, eight-foot. The plural, if you insist on being classical, is therefore *octopodes*, not *octopi*, which would be the plural of a Latin word that does not exist. *Octopuses* is fine. Everyone will understand you. I will not be issuing further rulings on plurals.

## The SI prefixes, which are a mess

The metric system's prefixes were supposed to be tidy: Greek for the multiples, Latin for the fractions.

| Multiple | Prefix | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | *deca-* | Greek *deka*, ten |
| 100 | *hecto-* | Greek *hekaton*, hundred |
| 1000 | *kilo-* | Greek *chilioi*, thousand |
| 10<sup>6</sup> | *mega-* | Greek *megas*, great |
| 10<sup>9</sup> | *giga-* | Greek *gigas*, giant |
| 10<sup>12</sup> | *tera-* | Greek *teras*, monster |
| 10<sup>15</sup> | *peta-* | Greek *penta* with a letter dropped |
| 10<sup>18</sup> | *exa-* | Greek *hexa* with a letter dropped |

| Fraction | Prefix | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| 1/10 | *deci-* | Latin *decimus*, tenth |
| 1/100 | *centi-* | Latin *centum*, hundred |
| 1/1000 | *milli-* | Latin *mille*, thousand |
| 10<sup>-6</sup> | *micro-* | Greek *mikros*, small (already breaking the rule) |
| 10<sup>-9</sup> | *nano-* | Greek *nanos*, dwarf |
| 10<sup>-12</sup> | *pico-* | Spanish or Italian *pico*, a small amount |
| 10<sup>-15</sup> | *femto-* | Danish *femten*, fifteen |
| 10<sup>-18</sup> | *atto-* | Danish *atten*, eighteen |

The Latin-for-fractions rule broke on the fourth step, and the multiples stopped being numbers after *kilo-* and started being adjectives. *Tera-* is a monster, which is a nice way to say "a trillion." *Femto-* and *atto-* are Danish, adopted in 1964, and the usual explanation is that the classical options had either run out or would have collided with existing symbols. The newest prefixes (*ronna-, quetta-, ronto-, quecto-*, adopted in 2022) were built backward from the only unused letters, r and q, with a vague nod to the Greek and Latin words for nine and ten.
