# 11. Impostors and the Etymological Fallacy

Two kinds of error remain. The first is seeing a root where there isn't one. The second is thinking that the root, when it is there, settles what the word means. Both deserve a chapter, because both are the failure modes of a person who has just learned what you have just learned.

## Words that look assembled but aren't

**Hamburger** contains no ham. It is a thing from Hamburg. The *-burger* got sawed off and reattached to everything, producing *cheeseburger*, which does contain cheese. **Sparrowgrass** is *asparagus* mangled by people who wanted it to mean something. **Female** is not *fe* + *male*; it is Latin *femella*, little woman, and its spelling was later bent toward *male* by people who assumed the two were related. **Island** has no *isle* in it: it is Old English *igland*, water-land, and the silent *s* was inserted in the sixteenth century by scholars who wrongly assumed it came from Latin *insula* (which is where *isle* comes from). They also stuck a *b* in *debt* and *doubt* to show off the Latin *debitum* and *dubitare*, and a *c* in *scissors* by false analogy with *scindere*. The silent letters in English are frequently scars from etymology done badly.

**Pen** and **pencil** are unrelated. *Pen* is *penna*, feather. *Pencil* is *penicillus*, a little brush, a diminutive of *peniculus*, a little tail, a diminutive of *penis*, which meant tail before it meant anything else. *Penicillin* is the same word, because the mold looks like a little brush.

**Belfry** has nothing to do with bells; it was *berfrey*, a siege tower, and the *l* crept in because bells got hung in them. **Crayfish** is not a fish; it is French *écrevisse*, reinterpreted. **Outrage** is not *out* + *rage*; it is French *outrage*, from Latin *ultra*, going beyond. **Sandblind** does not involve sand; it is Old English *sam-*, half. **Bridegroom** is *bryd* + *guma* (man), and *guma* died, so *groom* moved in. This process, where speakers rebuild an opaque word out of familiar parts, is called folk etymology, and it is the linguistic version of pareidolia: we see faces in the clouds and roots in the syllables.

**Pediatrics** and **pedestrian** share no root, as chapter five warned: Greek *paid-*, child, versus Latin *ped-*, foot. **Homosexual** and **Homo sapiens**: Greek *homos*, same, versus Latin *homo*, man. **Pan** the god and **pan-** the prefix are unrelated. **Antic** and **antique** are the same word, *antiquus*, old; *antic* came through Italian *antico*, which was applied to the grotesque figures found painted in excavated Roman ruins. But **anti-** the prefix is Greek for "against" and has nothing to do with either. And when you see *-ology* on the end of *tautology* and *cosmetology* and *Scientology*, only one of those is the study of anything.

The general rule: a root is confirmed when it appears in several words with related meanings, or when a dictionary says so. A single word that "looks like" it contains a root is a hypothesis, not a finding.

## The etymological fallacy

Now for the more insidious error. Suppose you have learned that *decimate* means "kill one in ten." You then encounter a news story saying that a storm decimated a town, and you feel a small warm glow of superiority: the writer means *devastated*, and does not know it. Was the writer wrong?

No. This is the etymological fallacy: the belief that a word's original meaning is its true meaning, and that later senses are corruptions. It is tempting for exactly the reason roots are useful: the original meaning is often illuminating. But illuminating is not the same as authoritative. Consider what the fallacy commits you to. *Nice* would mean ignorant. *Silly* would mean blessed. *Sinister* would mean left-handed. *Hysterical* would apply only to people with a uterus. A *dilapidated* building would have to be made of stone (*lapis*). You would have to *consider* only by looking at stars and be *sarcastic* only by tearing flesh. Nobody accepts this. So nobody actually believes the etymological fallacy as a principle; they only deploy it selectively, against words whose drift they happened to notice.

The philosophical point underneath is that meaning is not a hidden property of a word that etymology excavates. Meaning is what a word does in the mouths of the people who use it, and it changes when they change. Etymology tells you the history of that use, which is a different and wonderful thing. It tells you *why* the word is spelled as it is, what image it started from, what its relatives are. It gives you a strong prior about what an unfamiliar word means, which is the whole practical justification for this book. But when the prior conflicts with how a word is actually used, the usage is the data and the prior should update. *Decimate* now means to destroy a large part of. It has meant that in English since the seventeenth century, nearly as long as the word has existed in English at all, because the one-in-ten sense was mostly a historical term for a Roman practice, and English speakers rarely needed a word for that.

*Enormity* is a live version of the same fight. It comes from *e norma*, out of the norm, and originally meant a monstrous wickedness. Many people now use it to mean enormous size. The usage guides say this is an error; the corpus says it is at least as common as the old one. Give it fifty years.

The right attitude, I think, is this: learn the roots, enjoy the roots, use the roots to guess. Then let the guess be a guess. Anyone who tells you the "real" meaning of a word is what it meant in Latin is telling you that the past has authority over the present, and the whole history of English is a demonstration that it doesn't.
