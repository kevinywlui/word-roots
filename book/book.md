# The Fossil Record of English

## A short, opinionated guide to the Greek and Latin hiding in the words you already use

**Epistemic status:** Confident about the mechanics (how prefixes, roots, and suffixes snap together), which is settled and boring in the way settled things are. Moderately confident about the individual etymologies, which I have tried to restrict to ones the major dictionaries agree on; where a story is charming but shaky I say so. Not confident that learning roots will make you smarter, only that it will make reading feel less like being ambushed.

## How to read this

This is a short book, and it is designed to be read in order once and then raided. The first three chapters are the machinery: where the words came from, how they are assembled, and the few dozen prefixes that do most of the work. Chapters four through seven are a tour of the parts, with the best stories left in. The remaining chapters are about what goes wrong: words that entered twice, words whose meanings wandered, words that scientists made up, and words that look like they have roots but don't. The last chapter is the procedure for putting all of it to use, and the appendix holds the full lists and a cheat sheet.

You do not need to know any Latin or Greek. You need to know English, which, as it turns out, is mostly Latin and Greek wearing a Germanic coat.

# 1. English Is a Crime Scene

Here is a small experiment. Read this sentence:

> The king asked the cook for a hearty meal of bread, meat, and ale.

Now this one:

> The monarch requested a substantial repast of comestibles and beverages.

And this one:

> The potentate solicited nutritive alimentation.

All three mean roughly the same thing. But the first sounds like something a person would say, the second sounds like a hotel menu, and the third sounds like a hostage note written by a pharmacist. The difference is not grammar. The difference is that the first sentence is almost entirely Old English, the second is mostly Norman French (which is to say, Latin that spent a few centuries in France), and the third is Latin borrowed directly, or nearly so, by people who thought Latin was classy.

English has three layers, and every native speaker already feels them, the way you can feel the difference between *ask*, *question*, and *interrogate* without being able to say what it is. This book is about the top two layers. But to understand why they exist, you need the short version of how English got this way.

## Two invasions and a fad

**Layer one: the Germanic core.** The Angles, Saxons, and Jutes brought a West Germanic language to Britain in the fifth century. This layer gives us nearly all of the hundred most common words in English: *the, and, of, I, you, house, hand, eat, sleep, love, water, mother.* If you strip English down to what a toddler knows, it is almost pure Germanic. This is also why English feels so different from French despite sharing so much vocabulary: the skeleton is Germanic, and only the flesh is Latin.

**Layer two: Norman French.** In 1066, William of Normandy conquered England and installed a French-speaking aristocracy. For roughly three hundred years, the people who ran the courts, the church, the army, and the kitchen spoke French, and the people who did the actual work spoke English. You can still hear this. The animal in the field has a Germanic name because the peasant raised it: *cow, pig, sheep, deer.* The meat on the table has a French name because the lord ate it: *beef, pork, mutton, venison.* The Germanic word is *house*; the French word is *mansion*. The Germanic word is *ask*; the French word is *demand*. About ten thousand French words entered English in this period, and roughly three quarters of them are still in use. Since French is a direct descendant of Latin, this is Latin's first big entrance, though it came in disguised, with its consonants softened and its endings worn off.

**Layer three: the Renaissance and the inkhorn.** Starting in the sixteenth century, English scholars who had been educated in Latin and Greek decided English was impoverished and began importing classical words wholesale, straight from the source, no French intermediary. These were called "inkhorn terms" by people who hated them, on the grounds that they smelled of ink rather than speech. Some inkhorn terms died (nobody says *adnichilate* or *fatigate*). Many survived: *dexterity, expensive, encyclopedia, education, celebrate, dismiss.* This is why English so often has a plain French word and a fancier Latin word for the same concept: *frail* and *fragile* are literally the same Latin word, *fragilis*, borrowed twice, once through French in the Middle Ages and once directly in the Renaissance.

Then science happened, and science needed thousands of new words, and the convention was already set: you build them out of Greek and Latin parts. So the top layer kept growing and never stopped. *Television, photosynthesis, antibiotic, cardiovascular, neurotransmitter.* The word *hydrogen* is younger than the United States.

## Why this matters more than it seems

The result is a vocabulary in which the words you learn by living and the words you learn by reading come from different languages. Estimates vary, but a reasonable summary is that around 60 percent of English words have Latin or Greek origins, rising to about 90 percent in scientific and technical vocabulary, while the hundred most frequent words are about 95 percent Germanic.

This is the whole argument for learning roots, so it is worth stating carefully.

Word frequency in any language follows something like a Zipf distribution: a few words appear constantly and a very long tail appears rarely. You learn the head of the distribution for free, by talking to people. Nobody teaches you *the* or *hand*. The tail is different. The tail is where the vocabulary of medicine, law, philosophy, and every academic discipline lives, and the tail is overwhelmingly Latin and Greek. You cannot learn the tail by exposure, because by definition each word in it is rare.

But the tail is not random. It is assembled from a small kit. A few dozen prefixes, a few hundred roots, and a couple dozen suffixes account for an enormous share of it. If you know that *-itis* means inflammation, you do not need to have seen *tendinitis* or *bursitis* or *appendicitis* before; you need to know what a tendon, a bursa, and an appendix are. If you know that *bene-* means well and *dict* means say, then *benediction* is not a word you memorize but a word you compute.

This is unusually good leverage. Most vocabulary study is linear: one word learned, one word known. Root study is multiplicative. Learn *spec* (look) and you have a foothold in *inspect, spectacle, respect, suspect, speculate, perspective, conspicuous, spectrum, species,* and *retrospective*. Learn *-ology* and *-ologist* and you get a free suffix on every field of study that will ever exist.

The rest of this book is that kit, with the interesting parts left in.

# 2. How Words Are Assembled

A classical word in English is a sandwich: an optional prefix, a root, and an optional suffix. Sometimes two roots. Occasionally two prefixes. That is nearly the whole grammar, and if you internalize it you will start seeing seams in words you have used your whole life.

Take *incomprehensible*. It looks like a single sixteen-letter object. It is actually four parts:

| in- | com- | prehend | -ible |
|---|---|---|---|
| not | together | grasp | able to be |

"Not able to be grasped together." *Prehend* is the same root as in *prehensile* (a monkey's tail that grasps), *apprehend* (grasp toward, hence arrest), and *comprehend* (grasp fully). The suffix *-ible* is the same as *-able*; Latin had two spellings depending on the verb class, and English inherited both, which is why you have to memorize that it is *credible* but *dependable*. There is no rule. Sorry.

Once you start looking, the seams are everywhere. *Disruptive* is *dis-* (apart) + *rupt* (break) + *-ive* (tending to). *Rupt* is also in *erupt, interrupt, corrupt, bankrupt* (a broken bench, from the Italian moneylender's *banca rotta*), and *rupture*. *Transparent* is *trans-* (through) + *par* (appear) + *-ent* (doing). *Circumstance* is *circum-* (around) + *sta* (stand) + *-ance*: the things standing around you.

## The parts slide into each other

Latin did something that makes root-spotting harder: when a prefix ended in a consonant and the root began with one, the prefix consonant frequently changed to match. This is called assimilation, and it is why the same prefix wears so many costumes.

The prefix *ad-* (to, toward) is the champion. It appears as:

- *ad-* in *admit, advocate, adhere*
- *ac-* in *accept, accord, acquire*
- *af-* in *affect, affirm, affluent*
- *ag-* in *aggregate, aggressive*
- *al-* in *allocate, allude*
- *an-* in *announce, annex*
- *ap-* in *appear, apply, approve*
- *ar-* in *arrive, arrogant*
- *as-* in *assimilate, assume, assign*
- *at-* in *attract, attend, attempt*

The doubled consonant is the fingerprint. When you see an English word beginning with a vowel and then a doubled consonant, there is a good chance a prefix has melted into a root. *Accept* is *ad* + *capere* (take): take toward oneself. *Attract* is *ad* + *trahere* (drag). *Announce* is *ad* + *nuntiare* (report; a *nuncio* is a papal messenger).

The same thing happens with:

- *in-* (not, or in): *illegal, immobile, irregular, imbibe, illuminate*
- *con-* (with, together): *collect, combine, correct, cooperate* (and plain *co-* before vowels)
- *sub-* (under): *succeed, suffer, suggest, support, suspend* (with an extra *s*: *sus-*)
- *ob-* (against, toward): *occur, offer, oppose*
- *ex-* (out): *effect, efface,* and plain *e-* before many consonants, as in *emit, evade, elect*
- *dis-* (apart): *differ, diffuse,* and *di-* in *divert, digest*

Greek did it too. *Syn-* (together) becomes *syl-* in *syllable*, *sym-* in *symmetry* and *symphony*, and *sy-* in *system*. *En-* (in) becomes *em-* in *embryo* and *emphasis*.

The practical upshot: when a word begins with a short vowel followed by a double consonant, try peeling off a prefix. It works surprisingly often.

## The root itself changes shape

Latin verbs came in four "principal parts," and English borrowed from at least two of them, which is why one root can look like two. The verb *facere* (to make, do) has the stem *fac-* in *facile* and *facsimile*, but its past participle *factus* gives *fact* and *manufacture*, and when it followed a prefix the vowel weakened, giving *-fic-* in *efficient, deficit, magnificent* and *-fect-* in *perfect, infect, defect, effect, affect*. That is one verb, four spellings. Similarly:

- *capere* (take): *cap-, -cip-, capt-, -cept-*. *Capable, anticipate, captive, concept.*
- *tenere* (hold): *ten-, -tin-, tent-*. *Tenant, continent, content* (what is held in).
- *cadere* (fall): *cad-, -cid-, cas-*. *Cadence, accident, case.*
- *mittere* (send): *mitt-, miss-*. *Transmit, mission.*
- *scribere* (write): *scrib-, script-*. *Describe, description.*
- *videre* (see): *vid-, vis-*. *Evident, vision.*

You do not need to learn the rules of Latin vowel weakening. You need to know that it happened, so that when you see *-cip-* in *recipient* you think "probably *cap*, take," and you are right.

## Telling Greek from Latin

Why would you want to? Two reasons. First, Greek and Latin roots tend to combine with their own kind, so knowing which family a word belongs to narrows your guess. Second, Greek words cluster in science and medicine and Latin in law and administration, and the register tells you something.

The spelling tells are reliable:

- **ph, th, ch, rh, and y in the middle of a word are Greek.** *Philosophy, theater, chorus, rhythm, hypothesis, physics.* Latin had none of these letters; they were invented to transliterate Greek. (A *y* at the end of a word, as in *happy* or *reality*, means nothing; it is just an English spelling.)
- **Words ending in *-sis*, *-ma*, *-on*, or *-ic* skew Greek.** *Analysis, drama, phenomenon, cosmic.*
- **Words ending in *-tion*, *-ity*, *-ment*, *-ance*, or *-ous* skew Latin.** *Nation, quality, argument, tolerance, famous.*
- **The joining vowel.** When two Greek roots are glued together, the glue is usually *-o-*: *therm-o-meter, phil-o-sophy, psych-o-logy.* When two Latin roots are glued, it is usually *-i-*: *agr-i-culture, omn-i-potent, carn-i-vore.* This is one of the best tells there is.

That last rule also flags the hybrids: words with one Greek part and one Latin part, which purists once considered a kind of miscegenation. *Television* is Greek *tele* (far) plus Latin *visio* (seeing). C. P. Scott, editor of the *Manchester Guardian*, is reported to have said: "Television? The word is half Greek and half Latin. No good will come of it." *Automobile* is Greek *auto* plus Latin *mobilis*. *Sociology* is Latin *socius* plus Greek *logos*; when Auguste Comte coined it in the 1830s, critics sneered at the mongrel; the all-Greek alternative would have had to be built on *koinonia*, and nobody has ever wanted to say *koinonology*. Hybrids won. The purists lost. The language does not care.

## A note on what a "root" is

I am going to use the word "root" loosely, to mean any recurring chunk of a word that carries meaning. Linguists distinguish roots (the bare core) from stems (root plus some grammatical material) from combining forms (the version that shows up in compounds). For reading English, the distinctions rarely matter. What matters is being able to look at *photosynthesis* and see *phot* (light), *syn* (together), *the* (put; a different *the-* from the one in *theology*), *-sis* (process): the process of putting things together with light. Which is exactly what it is.

# 3. Prefixes: The Forty-Odd Words That Do All the Work

Prefixes are the highest-value items in the kit. There are only a few dozen, they are used constantly, and most of them mean something spatial, which makes them easy to picture: *in* and *out*, *over* and *under*, *before* and *after*, *through*, *around*, *against*. Most come in matched pairs, one Latin and one Greek: *super-* and *hyper-* both mean over, *circum-* and *peri-* both mean around, so a *circumference* and a *perimeter* are the same idea in two languages. The full set, grouped by meaning, is in the appendix. This chapter is about the ones with stories in them.

## Position and direction

**Ob-** means "in the way of," and it is hiding in some good words. *Obvious* is *ob* + *via*: in the road, right in front of you. *Obstacle* is *ob* + *stare*: something standing in the way. *Occur* is *ob* + *currere*: to run into.

**Per-** means "through" but also "thoroughly," and the second sense is worth knowing. *Perfect* is *per* + *factus*: thoroughly made, completely done. *Perjury* is to swear through and through, in the bad sense of "all the way to falsehood."

**Para-** in Greek meant "beside," and it drifted into "beside the normal," hence "faulty" or "beyond." A *paradox* is *para* + *doxa*: beside received opinion. A *parasite* is *para* + *sitos* (food): one who eats beside you, at your table, a professional dinner guest, which is what the word originally meant in Greek before biology got hold of it.

**Apo-** means "away from," and gives a nice cluster of religious words. An *apostle* is one sent away. An *apocalypse* is an uncovering, *apo* + *kalyptein* (to cover), which is why the last book of the New Testament is also called Revelation; they are the same word in two languages. *Apology*, from *apo* + *logos*, was originally a speech made in one's own defense; Plato's *Apology* is Socrates defending himself, not saying sorry.

**Epi-** means "upon." An *epidemic* is a disease upon the people (*demos*); a *pandemic* is upon all the people; an *endemic* disease is one that lives in the people. The *epicenter* of an earthquake is the point on the surface directly upon the actual center, which is underground and called the *hypocenter*. When journalists use "epicenter" to mean "the center," they are technically pointing at the wrong spot.

**Meta-** deserves its own paragraph because it has become so fashionable. In Greek it meant "after" or "beyond" or "change." *Metamorphosis* is a change of form. The modern sense of "about itself, one level up" comes from an accident of library organization: when Aristotle's works were arranged in the first century BC, the untitled books that came after the *Physics* were labeled *ta meta ta physika*, "the ones after the physics." Those books happened to be about first principles and the nature of being. So *metaphysics* came to mean "the study of what lies beyond the physical," and *meta-* came to mean "above and about," and now we have metadata and metacognition and people saying "that's so meta." All from a shelving decision.

## Quantity and quality

**The two *in-* prefixes.** Latin had two prefixes that both came out as *in-*. One means "not" (*invisible*). The other means "in, into," and often just intensifies (*inflame*). Usually context sorts it out. Sometimes it doesn't. *Inflammable* uses the second one: it means "able to be set aflame," the same as *flammable*. Enough people read it as "not flammable" that the word *flammable* was actively promoted by fire-safety organizations in the twentieth century to replace it on warning labels. *Invaluable* uses the first: "not able to be valued," so precious it is beyond pricing. *Inhabitable* uses the second again: "able to be lived in," not "un-habitable." If a word beginning with *in-* seems to contradict itself, one of the two prefixes is usually the culprit.

**Greek *a-* (or *an-* before a vowel)** is the "alpha privative," and it is one of the most productive negatives in scientific English. *Atom* (uncuttable), *anonymous* (no name), *anesthesia* (no sensation), *atrophy* (no nourishment). When you see an *a-* on the front of a Greek-looking word, try "without."

**Dys-** versus **dis-.** They sound identical, and they are unrelated. Greek *dys-* means bad or difficult: *dyslexia* (bad reading), *dystopia* (bad place). Latin *dis-* means apart or not: *dismiss, disconnect*.

*Dystopia* has a good origin. Thomas More coined *Utopia* in 1516 from Greek *ou* (not) + *topos* (place): no-place, nowhere. But it was also a pun on *eu-topos*, good place, and the pun is why the word came to mean "ideal society." When John Stuart Mill wanted an opposite in 1868, he reached for *dys-* and said *dystopia* in Parliament. He was not quite the first (a stray *dustopia* turns up in 1747), but he is the reason the word stuck, and in using it he quietly confirmed that everyone had been hearing the pun all along.

**Hyper-** and **hypo-** are the ones most likely to matter in a hospital. *Hyper* is over, *hypo* is under. *Hyperthermia* is too hot, *hypothermia* is too cold. *Hypoglycemia* is too little sugar in the blood. *Hypothesis* is something placed under, a foundation you build an argument on. The only trick is remembering which is which, and the mnemonic I use is that *hyper* has the higher-sounding vowel. It is not a good mnemonic. It works anyway.

# 4. The Latin Verbs You Already Conjugate

Latin verbs are the workhorses of formal English. A couple dozen of them, each with a few spellings and each combining with the prefixes from the last chapter, generate several hundred everyday words. This chapter is a tour of the best stories in the most important of them. Read it for the connections; the full word families are in the appendix, for when you want to raid them.

## Make, take, carry

**FAC / FIC / FACT / FECT: make, do.** From *facere*, possibly the single most productive root in English. A *factory* is where things are made; a *fact* is a thing done. A *facsimile* is "make it similar," and *fax* is what happens when you say that quickly. *Satisfy* is *satis* (enough) + *facere*: to do enough. And *affect* and *effect* are the same root with different prefixes (to do something *to*, and what is made *out*), which is either a consolation or an insult to everyone who confuses them.

**CAP / CIP / CAPT / CEPT: take, seize.** From *capere*: *capture, accept, receive*. A *recipe* is the Latin imperative "take!", the first word of a prescription. My favorite is *emancipate*: *e* + *manus* (hand) + *capere*, to take out of someone's hand, from a Roman legal ceremony for releasing a son from his father's power.

**PORT: carry.** From *portare*: *portable, export, transport*. *Opportunity* is *ob* + *portus*, harbor: a wind carrying you toward port, a favorable moment. And *sport* is *disport*, to carry oneself away from work.

**FER / LAT: carry, bear.** From *ferre*, and here is a wonderful irregularity. Latin *ferre* borrowed its past participle from a completely different verb, so the past participle of "carry" was *latum*. English inherited both halves without telling anyone they belonged together. So *refer* and *relate* are the same verb: carry back. *Transfer* and *translate*: carry across (a translation carries meaning across languages; a bishop *translated* to a new see has been carried across). *Confer* and *collate*: carry together. And a *legislator* is *legis* (of the law) + *lator* (bearer): one who carries the law.

## See, speak, send

**SPEC / SPECT: look.** From *specere*: *inspect, respect, spectator*. *Auspicious* is from *avis* (bird) + *specere*: watching birds for omens, an *auspex* being the Roman official who did so. To *speculate* was to watch from a lookout tower, a *specula*, and only later to theorize or to gamble on the market. And *species* was just the look of a thing, its kind, until Linnaeus made it technical.

**VID / VIS: see.** From *videre*. *Video* is simply Latin for "I see." To *provide* is to see ahead, and *providence*, contracted, became *prudence*: foresight. *Improvise* is the reverse, the unforeseen, done on the spot. And *envy* is *invidere*, to look at with hostility, the evil eye; *invidious* still has the sting.

**DIC / DICT: say.** From *dicere*: *dictionary, predict, contradict*. A *verdict* is a true saying, from *verus*. An *addict* was originally a person given over to someone by a court's decree, long before it meant a person given over to a habit.

**MIT / MISS: send.** From *mittere*: *mission, missile, transmit, dismiss*. A *promise* is something sent forward, a word sent ahead of the deed, and a *compromise* is a promise made mutually.

## Stand, hold, bind

**STA / STAT / SIST: stand.** This root has the distinction of being the same root in Latin, Greek, and English; *stand*, *stasis*, and *status* are cousins going back six thousand years to a Proto-Indo-European verb. A *solstice* is the day the sun stands still; an *armistice* is a standing-still of arms. *Ecstasy* is the Greek version, *ek* + *stasis*: standing outside yourself. *Superstition* is standing over something, though nobody is certain what the original image was; the best guess is standing over it in awe. And a *stable* is where the horses stand.

**TEN / TENT / TAIN: hold.** From *tenere*: *tenant, contain, retain*. A *tenet* is Latin for "he holds." A *continent* is land that holds together. A *lieutenant* is one holding a place (*lieu*) for a superior; the British pronounce it "leftenant" for reasons nobody has satisfactorily explained.

**STRING / STRICT: draw tight, bind.** From *stringere*: *strict, restrict, stress*. *Prestige* is the surprise: from *praestigiae*, a conjurer's tricks, from *praestringere*, to bind fast or blindfold. A person of prestige was originally one who dazzled by illusion. The word meant "deception" until the nineteenth century, when it turned into a compliment.

## Go and come

**CED / CESS: go, yield.** From *cedere*: *proceed, recede, exceed*. An *ancestor* is from *antecessor*, one who went before. And *necessary* is probably *ne* + *cedere*: what does not yield, what cannot be got out of the way.

**VEN / VENT: come.** From *venire*: *convene, event, venue*. To *invent* was to come upon something, to find it. To *prevent* was to come before, to anticipate; it only later came to mean to stop. And a *souvenir* is what comes back to mind.

That is thirteen verbs and a few stories each. Add *duc* (lead), *pon* (put), *jac* (throw), *pel* (drive), *voc* (call), and a few others from the appendix, and you can decompose a large fraction of the abstract vocabulary of English on sight.

# 5. Heads, Hands, Hearts, and Feet

Latin nouns entered English mostly as the second half of compounds or as the base of adjectives. The body parts are the best place to start, because they are concrete, they are everywhere, and they show off the strangest feature of the whole system: how a single Latin word can arrive in English three or four separate times with a different face each time.

## CAPUT: head

*Caput* (stem *capit-*) meant head. Directly from Latin we get *capital* (the head city; the head sum of money on which interest accrues), *decapitate*, and *per capita* (by heads). To *capitulate* was to draw up terms under headings, and so to surrender. A *chapter* is a *capitulum*, a little head. So, oddly, is a *cabbage*, via French *caboche*, "big head."

Now watch what French does. Latin *ca-* at the start of a word turned into *cha-* in Parisian French but stayed *ca-* in the Norman dialect. So *caput* came into English as *chief* (Parisian: the head man) and later again as *chef* (the head of the kitchen, re-borrowed in the nineteenth century with its French pronunciation intact). *Capital* came through Norman French as *cattle* and through Parisian French as *chattel*, and both meant "movable property." In a medieval economy where wealth was livestock, "capital" and "cattle" were the same thing, and the language remembers it. *Captain* is a head man; a *cape* is a headland. That is one Latin noun, at least ten English words, and three different pronunciations of the first syllable.

## MANUS: hand

*Manual, manufacture, manuscript*: all done by hand. To *manipulate* is to handle, from *manipulus*, a handful, which was also the word for a Roman army unit small enough to be handled. And *manure* is the same word as *maneuver*, both from *manu operare*, to work by hand; a farmer manured a field by working it, and the stuff he spread on it took the name.

## PES / PED: foot

*Pedal, pedestrian, centipede.* To *impede* is to put the feet in fetters; to *expedite* is to free them, and so to speed things up. A *pedigree* is French *pied de grue*, crane's foot, from the branching shape of a genealogical diagram. The *pawn* in chess is the foot soldier, and a *pioneer* was a foot soldier sent ahead to dig.

Not to be confused with Greek *paid-* (child), spelled *ped-* or *paed-* in English, in *pediatrics* and *pedagogy*. Those are unrelated, and a pediatrician is not a foot doctor.

## COR and CORPUS: heart and body

*Cordial* is from the heart, and *courage* is *cor* through French. To *record* is *re* + *cor*: to call back to the heart, to learn by heart, which is what recording was before machines. My favorite is *misericord*, a heart of mercy, which is also the name of the little ledge on a choir stall that lets a monk rest while appearing to stand.

*Corpus* gives *corpse*, *corps* (a body of troops), and *corporation* (a body of people treated as a legal person). *Habeas corpus* is "you shall have the body," the writ demanding a prisoner be produced. But a military *corporal* is not bodily at all; that one is from *caput* via Italian *caporale*, a head man, and got bent toward *corpus* by resemblance.

## ANIMA and MENS: soul and mind

*Anima* is breath and soul, hence *animal*: a breathing thing. The soul words are the good ones: *unanimous* (of one mind), *magnanimous* (great-souled), and its glorious opposite *pusillanimous*, tiny-souled. *Mens* is mind: *mental*, *dementia* (mind gone away), and *memento*, the Latin imperative "remember!" The Greek counterpart, *psyche*, gave us *psychedelic*, mind-manifesting, coined in 1956 by the psychiatrist Humphry Osmond in a letter to Aldous Huxley.

## LUX and CANDERE: light and white

*Lux* gives *lucid* and *Lucifer*, the light-bearer, originally the morning star, which is Venus. And *candere*, to glow white, gives *candle*, *incandescent*, *candid* (white, pure, hence frank), and *candidate*: a Roman seeking office wore a specially whitened toga, a *toga candida*, so he was a *candidatus*, a whitened man. The gap between the etymology and the modern politician is left as an exercise.

## Earth, water, fire, and sky

The elements are mostly straightforward (*terrain, aquarium, ignite, marine, solar*), with a few treats. The *Mediterranean* is the sea in the middle of the lands. A *terrier* is a dog that goes into the earth after its prey. A *lunatic* is moonstruck. And from *sidus* (stem *sider-*, star), *consider* may once have meant to examine the stars, an augural term, and *desire* may be *de sidere*, "from the stars," to await what the stars will bring. Both are pretty and both are disputed.

The Greek parallels are *geo-* (earth), *hydro-* (water), *pyro-* (fire), *helio-* (sun), and *astro-* (star), and chapter seven is about why Greek got the science and Latin got the law.

# 6. Counting in Two Dead Languages

Numbers are the tidiest part of the kit, and they also contain the most famous scandal in the English calendar. Latin and Greek each had a full set, and English uses both, often side by side: a *monopoly* and a *unicorn*, a *pentagon* and a *quintet*, a *kilogram* and a *millennium*. The counting is easy and the table is in the appendix. The stories are better.

**The calendar is off by two.** *September, October, November, December* are the seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth months by their names, and the ninth, tenth, eleventh, and twelfth by the calendar. The reason is that the early Roman year began in March; January and February were added at the end and later moved to the front. The names never got fixed. (*July* and *August* were *Quintilis* and *Sextilis*, fifth and sixth, until they were renamed for Julius and Augustus Caesar. Nobody dared rename the rest.)

**A mile is a thousand.** The word is *mille passus*, a thousand paces, and a Roman *passus* was a double step, left foot to left foot, about five feet. A Roman mile was therefore about five thousand feet, and the modern mile of 5,280 feet is a sixteenth-century English compromise with the furlong.

**Decimate means one in ten.** The Roman army punished a mutinous unit by executing one soldier in ten, chosen by lot. So *decimate* originally meant to reduce by a tenth. Today it means to destroy most of something, and there is a certain kind of person who will correct you about this. We will discuss whether that person is right in chapter eleven. (Short answer: no.)

**Dilemma is Greek, not Latin.** *Di* + *lemma*, two premises: an argument that traps you between two conclusions. The horns of a dilemma were a later, extremely good metaphor.

**Quarantine is forty.** From Italian *quaranta giorni*, forty days, the period Venice held ships arriving from plague ports, from the fifteenth century; Latin *quadraginta* underneath. The number has since become negotiable.

**Sesquipedalian.** *Sesqui* (one and a half) + *ped* (foot): a foot-and-a-half long. Horace used *sesquipedalia verba*, "foot-and-a-half-long words," as a joke about pompous poets, and English borrowed the joke intact. It is the only self-describing word in this book.

**Octopus.** It is Greek: *okto* + *pous*, eight-foot. The plural, if you insist on being classical, is therefore *octopodes*, not *octopi*, which would be the plural of a Latin word that does not exist. *Octopuses* is fine. Everyone will understand you. I will not be issuing further rulings on plurals.

## The SI prefixes, which are a mess

The metric system's prefixes were supposed to be tidy: Greek for the multiples, Latin for the fractions.

The newest prefixes (*ronna-, quetta-, ronto-, quecto-*, adopted in 2022) were built backward from the only unused letters, r and q, with a vague nod to the Greek and Latin words for nine and ten.

# 7. Greek: The Language Science Speaks

Latin got the law, the church, and the administration because Latin was the language of the Roman state and then of the medieval church. Greek got science and medicine for a slightly different reason: the Greeks got there first. Hippocrates, Aristotle, Euclid, Galen, and Ptolemy wrote in Greek, and when the Renaissance rediscovered them, their vocabulary came along. Then, when the scientific revolution needed thousands of new words, Greek had the advantage of being a compounding language: you could stick two or three roots together and get something that sounded like a word. Latin could do it too, but Greek did it more naturally, and the habit stuck. By the nineteenth century, if you discovered something, you named it in Greek.

This chapter is a handful of the Greek roots that matter most, and the stories hiding in them. The fuller families, the medical kit, and the long tail of smaller roots are in the appendix.

## LOGOS: word, reason, study

*Logos* is the most important Greek root in English, and it means at least three things: word, reason, and account. The suffix *-logy* means "the study of," and it attaches to everything: *biology, geology, theology*, and any field you care to invent. *Etymology* is the study of the *etymon*, the true meaning of a word. *-logue* is a spoken thing: a *prologue* is the speech before, an *epilogue* the speech upon, after. A *eulogy* is good speech about someone, usually when they can no longer hear it. And *logistics* is probably not family at all; it is from French *loger*, to lodge, though Greek *logistikos*, skilled in reckoning, may have helped it along.

## GRAPH / GRAM: write, draw

*Graphein* meant to scratch, then to write, then to draw: *autograph, biography, geography*. *Photograph*, light-writing, was coined by John Herschel in 1839. A *paragraph* was originally a mark written beside the text to show a new section, not the section itself. A *program* is *pro* + *gramma*: something written up beforehand, a public notice.

*Grammar* has one of the best histories in the language. In the Middle Ages, *grammar* meant learning in general, particularly Latin, which to the illiterate looked indistinguishable from sorcery. So *gramarye* came to mean occult knowledge, and in Scots dialect the word was worn down to *glamour*, meaning a spell or enchantment. Walter Scott popularized it in the early nineteenth century, and by the twentieth century it meant the kind of enchantment Hollywood sells. *Glamour* and *grammar* are the same word, and both, in their way, are still about the power of knowing the right form.

## PATH: feeling, suffering

*Pathos* is suffering, experience, feeling: *sympathy* is feeling with, *apathy* is no feeling, *pathology* is the study of suffering, hence of disease. *Empathy*, feeling into, was coined in 1909 by the psychologist Edward Titchener to translate the German *Einfühlung*, so it is one of the newest words in this chapter. And *pathetic* originally just meant "affecting"; now it is a sneer.

## TOM: cut

*Temnein*, to cut, gives one of the most elegant clusters in English. An *atom* is *a* + *tomos*: uncuttable, the thing you reach when you can't divide any further. Democritus proposed the concept around 400 BC; it took about 2,300 years to find out he was wrong and it could be cut after all, and by then the name had stuck. *Anatomy* is cutting up. *Dichotomy* is cutting in two. Every *-ectomy* is a cutting out. An *epitome* was literally a cutting-upon, an abridgment, a summary; the modern sense of "the perfect example" came from the idea of a work that contains the whole in small. *Entomology* is the study of insects, from *entomon*, "cut-in," because insects have bodies cut into segments, and *insect* is the Latin translation of exactly that: *in* + *secare*, cut into. Two languages, one image, one bug.

## ERG / URG: work

*Ergon*, work, is the root of *energy* (Aristotle's *energeia*, being-at-work), *synergy* (working together), and *allergy* (*allos* + *ergon*: other-working; coined in 1906 by Clemens von Pirquet for a changed reaction). It is also, less obviously, *surgery*. That one is *cheir* (hand) + *ergon*: hand-work. A surgeon was originally a *chirurgeon*, and the word was worn down through French; a *chiropractor* is a hand-practitioner from the same *cheir*. The name *George* is *ge* + *ergon*, earth-worker, farmer. And *orgy*, from *orgia*, is probably the same root: secret rites, "works" performed for a god, which subsequently got a reputation.

## -CRACY and -ARCHY: rule

Greek had two words for power: *kratos*, strength, and *arche*, beginning or first place. *Democracy* is people-power; *aristocracy* is rule by the best; *kakistocracy*, a word that deserves more use, is rule by the worst. *Bureaucracy* is rule by desks, from French *bureau*. *Meritocracy* was first recorded in 1956 and made famous in 1958 by Michael Young, who used it as a term of abuse in a satirical novel; it is now used unironically, which would have annoyed him. On the *-archy* side, *hierarchy* was originally rule by priests (*hieros*, sacred); the modern sense of ranked layers comes from a fifth-century theologian who described the ranks of angels.

## The medical kit

Medicine is where Greek is most systematic, and it rewards learning as a set. A dozen suffixes (*-itis* inflammation, *-ectomy* removal, *-emia* blood condition, *-algia* pain) and a couple dozen body parts (*hepat-* liver, *nephr-* kidney, *oste-* bone, *rhin-* nose) combine freely. Once you have them, *hepatitis, nephrology, osteosarcoma,* and *rhinoplasty* are all words you can read without ever having seen them. The kit is in the appendix. One member deserves a mention here: *nostalgia* is *-algia* too, the pain of homecoming, and chapter twelve has its surprisingly clinical history.

## GEN: birth, kind

*Genesis, gene, generate.* To be *generous* was to be of noble birth, and so noble in behavior. A *genius* was a guardian spirit born with you. An *engine* is from *ingenium*, inborn talent, which is why an *engineer* and an *ingenious* person are close relatives. *Hydrogen* is water-producer and *oxygen* is acid-producer, and chapter ten explains why the second one was a mistake. Latin and Greek share this root, which is why *genus* (Latin) and *genesis* (Greek) sit side by side, and English *kin* and *kind* are the Germanic cousins.

## A few more worth knowing

A handful of the smaller roots have stories too good to leave in the appendix. *Cosm-* is order: the Greek verb *kosmein* meant both "to order" and "to adorn," so the *cosmos* is the universe considered as a beautifully arranged thing, and *cosmetic* is the same word. *Astr-* is star, and a *disaster* is a bad star. *The-* is god, and *enthusiasm* is having a god inside you. *Hydrophobia*, fear of water, was the old name for rabies. And a *horoscope* is an hour-watcher.

If the Latin chapters felt like learning the internals of the civil service, this one should feel like learning the internals of a hospital and a university, and that is roughly the division of labor the two languages settled on.

# 8. Words That Came Twice

The French layer and the direct-from-Latin layer overlap. Many Latin words entered English through Old French in the Middle Ages, wearing French sound changes, and then entered again in the Renaissance straight from the Latin dictionary. The two arrivals settled in as separate words, usually with the French one meaning something everyday and the Latin one meaning something formal. Linguists call these doublets, and they are the best evidence that sound change is lawful rather than random.

| Latin | Early, worn down | Later, closer to Latin |
|---|---|---|
| *fragilis* | frail | fragile |
| *regalis* | royal | regal |
| *legalis* | loyal | legal |
| *securus* | sure | secure |
| *ratio* | reason | ratio |
| *potio* | poison | potion |
| *cadentia* | chance | cadence |
| *castellum* | castle | (none; *chateau* is a later French re-borrowing) |
| *dignitas* | dainty | dignity |
| *hospitale* | hostel, hotel | hospital |
| *traditio* | treason | tradition |
| *historia* | story | history |
| *species* | spice | species |
| *corpus* | corpse, corps | corpus |
| *separare* | sever | separate |
| *pauper* | poor | pauper |
| *blasphemare* | blame | blaspheme |
| *thesaurus* (Greek) | treasure | thesaurus |
| *paralysis* (Greek) | palsy | paralysis |
| *episcopus* (Greek) | bishop | episcopal |
| *presbyter* (Greek) | priest | presbyter |

The last two came through Old English rather than French, but the wearing-down is the same. Some of these deserve a sentence.

**Treason and tradition** are both *traditio*, a handing-over. You can hand over a body of custom to the next generation, or hand over a city to the enemy. A *traitor* is a *traditor*, one who hands over. Early Christians used *traditor* for those who handed the scriptures to Roman persecutors, and the word never recovered.

**Poison and potion** are both *potio*, a drink. The difference is what's in it. *Potable* is from the same verb.

**Spice and species.** Latin *species* meant appearance, then kind or sort, and in Late Latin, "goods," specifically the sorted merchandise a trader dealt in. Via Old French *espice*, this became *spice*: the goods par excellence, the ones worth sailing around Africa for.

**Story and history** are both Greek *historia* (inquiry) through Latin. *History* kept the sense of inquiry into the past; *story* drifted toward narrative of any kind. The historian and the storyteller are the same word, which is a fact both professions might prefer to keep quiet.

**Blame and blaspheme.** *Blasphemein*, to speak evil of, entered Old French as *blasmer* and came out as *blame*. To blame someone is, etymologically, to blaspheme them. The direct borrowing kept its theological register.

**Dainty and dignity.** *Dignitas*, worth. A dainty was originally a thing of worth, a delicacy; the adjective followed. Both words mean "worthy," but one is a duchess and the other is a petit four.

**Chance and cadence.** *Cadere*, to fall. A chance is how the dice fall; a cadence is how the voice falls at the end of a phrase. *Accident* (a falling-toward), *decadence* (a falling away), *coincide*, *deciduous* (falling off), *case* (how things fall out, and the grammatical case; the case that holds things is a different word), *casual, cascade, occasion* (a falling-toward, an opportunity), and *cadaver* (the fallen) are all the same fall.

## Why the French forms look the way they do

The sound changes are consistent enough that you can learn to see through them. Three rules cover most of the territory.

**Latin *ca-* becomes French *cha-*.** Chapter five showed this with *cattle* and *chattel*; here is the general rule. *Caput* to *chief*, *castellum* to *chateau*, *cantare* to *chant*, *carus* to *cher* (and *cherish*), *capitulum* to *chapter*, *cattus* to *chat*. Norman French, the dialect that actually invaded England, did not make this change, which is why English sometimes has both: *cattle* and *chattel*, *catch* and *chase* (both from *captiare*, to seize), and *castle* and *chateau*.

**Latin *-tion* stays; Latin *-atus* becomes *-é* or *-ee*.** *Nation, station, ration* look Latin because French kept them nearly intact. But the past participle *-atus* became *-é*, and English took it as *-ee* in *employee, referee, trustee, refugee*: one to whom the action is done. The doer got *-er* or *-or*. So *employer* and *employee* encode a Latin voice distinction through a French filter, which is more grammar than most people know they're using.

**Latin *p* between vowels becomes French *v*.** *Sapere* (to taste, to be wise) gives French *savoir*, and its noun *sapor* gives English *savor*; the direct Latin borrowing gives *sapient* and *Homo sapiens*. *Ripa* (riverbank) gives *river* through French; the Latin form gives *riparian*. *Capra* (goat) gives *chevron* through French (originally a rafter; the Romans called rafters little goats) and *Capricorn* directly.

You do not need to memorize these. But once you have seen a few, you start to recognize the two costumes, and to guess that a plain-sounding English word with a soft *ch* or a missing consonant has a starchier Latin twin somewhere in the dictionary. Usually it does.

# 9. Semantic Drift, or Why Nice Meant Stupid

Knowing a word's parts tells you what it meant when it was assembled. It does not tell you what it means now, because meanings drift, and they drift in predictable directions. This chapter is about the drift, partly because it is fun and partly because it is the thing that makes root-based guessing fail, and you should know when to expect failure.

## The direction of drift

There are a few standard patterns.

**Narrowing.** *Meat* once meant food in general and *deer* once meant any animal; both are Germanic, but the pattern is universal. Latin *species* narrowed from "kind" to a biological rank, and *doctor* from "teacher" to "physician."

**Broadening.** *Decimate* broadened from "kill one in ten" to "devastate." *Virtue* broadened from *virtus*, manliness, courage, to goodness in general, and then narrowed again to chastity in particular contexts.

**Pejoration** (getting worse). This is common, and it happens to words for women, servants, and peasants with depressing regularity. *Villain* is from *villanus*, a farm worker at a *villa*. *Silly* (Germanic) went from blessed to innocent to pitiable to foolish. *Pathetic* went from "moving" to "contemptible." *Notorious* went from "well known" to "well known for something bad." *Egregious* went from *e grege*, standing out from the flock, exceptional, to exceptionally bad. *Sinister* is just Latin for "left," and the superstition that the left hand is unlucky did the rest.

**Amelioration** (getting better). Rarer. *Nice* is the classic. From *nescius*, ignorant (*ne* + *scire*, not knowing; the same root as *science*), it meant foolish in the thirteenth century, then fussy, then precise (a "nice distinction" still means a fine one), then pleasant. It took some five hundred years to travel from an insult to a compliment, and it spent most of that time meaning something like "overly particular." *Prestige* went from trickery to renown (chapter four). *Shrewd* went from wicked to sharp. *Terrific* went from terrifying to great, and *awesome* is doing the same thing right now.

**Concrete to abstract.** This is the big one, and it is the source of most of the surprises in etymology. Abstract words are almost always dead metaphors: a concrete image that got used figuratively until nobody remembered the image. To *understand* was (probably) to stand among. To *comprehend* was to grasp. To *consider* was (probably) to look at the stars. *Abstract* itself is *abs* + *tractus*: dragged away.

## A short tour of dead metaphors

Some of these are so good they justify the whole enterprise.

**Muscle** is *musculus*, little mouse, because a flexing biceps looks like a mouse moving under the skin. The Greeks had the same idea: *mys* meant both mouse and muscle, and gives us *myalgia* and *myocardium*.

**Pupil** (of the eye) is *pupilla*, little doll, because you see a tiny reflected figure of yourself in someone's eye. **Pupil** (the student) is the same word in the sense of a little one, a ward. Both come from *pupa*, a girl or doll, which is also the name for the stage of an insect's life when it looks like a swaddled infant.

**Calculus** is a pebble. Romans counted with pebbles on a board, so *calculare* meant to reckon. A kidney stone is also a calculus, and that sense is older than the mathematics. Newton and Leibniz's calculus is "the pebble-reckoning," a name it got from the phrase *calculus differentialis*.

**Salary** is from *salarium*, which is from *sal*, salt. The popular story is that Roman soldiers were paid in salt. The evidence for this is weak; the more likely account is that the *salarium* was an allowance for buying salt, or a payment named after salt because salt was the paradigm of a necessary purchase. Either way, the salt is real, and "worth his salt" is a translation of the etymology.

**Trivial** is *trivium*, a place where three roads meet. What you find at a crossroads is common, public, ordinary gossip, hence *trivialis*, commonplace. Separately, the medieval *trivium* was the first three liberal arts (grammar, logic, rhetoric), the introductory course before the *quadrivium*; that sense reinforced the "elementary" flavor.

**Companion** is *com* + *panis*, with-bread: someone you share bread with. *Company* is the same. The Germanic word *lord* is *hlaf-weard*, loaf-guardian, and *lady* is *hlaf-dige*, loaf-kneader. Bread turns out to be the foundation of social structure in both languages.

**Sarcasm** is from *sarkazein*, to tear flesh like a dog, from *sarx*, flesh. A *sarcophagus* is a flesh-eater: the Greeks believed a certain limestone dissolved bodies placed in it, and used it for coffins. *Sarcoma* is a tumor of the flesh.

**Candidate** we covered: a man in a whitened toga. **Ambition** is *ambire*, to go around, specifically to go around canvassing for votes. Roman politics gave us both words, and *ambition*, at least, was not originally a compliment.

**Curfew** is French *couvre-feu*, cover-fire: the evening bell that told you to bank your hearth. **Garble** is from Arabic via Italian, to sift spices, and meant to sort until it meant to mix up.

## The astrology layer

Which brings up a whole stratum of English that most people walk past. Medieval and Renaissance Europe took astrology seriously, and the vocabulary of astrology settled into ordinary English as the belief evaporated.

*Influence* is *influentia*, an in-flowing: the ethereal fluid that streamed from the stars and shaped your character. When Chaucer used it, he meant the astral kind. *Consider* and *desire* may be sidereal, as chapter five mentioned. *Disaster* is *dis* + *astrum*, a bad star, via Italian *disastro*. *Lunatic* is one afflicted by the moon. *Jovial* is born under Jupiter (Jove), and so cheerful; *saturnine* under Saturn, and so gloomy; *mercurial* under Mercury, and so changeable; *martial* under Mars, and so warlike; *venereal* under Venus, and so, well. A *sanguine* temperament, a *melancholy* one (Greek: black bile), a *phlegmatic* one, a *choleric* one: the four humors, another abandoned theory that left its whole vocabulary behind.

This is worth noticing as a general pattern: a theory dies, and its words keep walking around. We still say the sun *rises*. We still call it *hysteria* (from *hystera*, womb; the theory was that it was a disease of the uterus). We say someone is in *good humor*. *Cretin* is from a Swiss French dialect word for Christian, applied to people with congenital thyroid disease as a reminder that they were human. The fossil record of English includes the fossils of ideas.

## The mythology layer

The same for the gods. *Panic* is from Pan, whose shout terrified armies. *Tantalize* is from Tantalus, condemned to stand in water he could not drink. *Atlas*, the book, is from Atlas holding up the sky, because Mercator put him on the title page. *Hypnosis* is Hypnos, sleep. *Morphine* is Morpheus, dreams. *Nemesis* was the goddess of retribution. *Cereal* is Ceres. *Volcano* is Vulcan. *Museum* is a temple of the Muses, and *music* is their art. *Hermetic* is from Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary alchemist, and means sealed because he was believed to have invented a way of sealing vessels. *Fortune, flora, fauna, cupidity, erotic, narcissism, echo, mentor, chaos, titanic, protean, herculean, odyssey, siren, nectar, ambrosia.* The Greeks and Romans stopped worshipping their gods but never stopped using their names, and neither have we.

The lesson for the reader of unknown words is this: decomposition gives you the original picture, and the original picture is usually a good hint. But the current meaning is wherever the drift stopped, and when the parts and the word disagree, the word wins.

# 10. The Neologism Factory

Most of the Latin and Greek in English arrived organically, through conquest and scholarship. But a large and growing share was manufactured: coined on purpose, by identifiable people, on identifiable dates, out of classical parts. Science is the main factory, and the way scientists name things tells you a lot about both the science and the scientists.

## Chemistry: named for what they thought it did

The chemical elements are a museum of eighteenth-century assumptions. Antoine Lavoisier, who more or less invented modern chemistry in the 1780s, named two gases. *Hydrogen* is *hydro* + *gen*, water-producer, because burning it makes water. Correct. *Oxygen* is *oxy* (sharp, acid) + *gen*, acid-producer, because Lavoisier believed all acids contained oxygen. Wrong: hydrochloric acid has none. The name outlived the theory by two centuries and counting. The German word *Sauerstoff*, "sour stuff," is the same mistake translated.

The noble gases were named in a burst in the 1890s by William Ramsay, and he named them for the experience of discovering them. *Argon* is *argos*, lazy, because it would not react with anything. *Neon* is *neos*, new. *Krypton* is *kryptos*, hidden. *Xenon* is *xenos*, stranger. *Helium* had been named earlier, in 1868, from *helios*, sun, because it was first detected in the solar spectrum during an eclipse, before anyone had found it on Earth. The *-ium* ending is the Latin ending for metals (*sodium, potassium, calcium*), and the astronomers who named it assumed it was one. It is not, and it is the only noble gas with a metal's name.

*Technetium* is *technetos*, artificial: the first element made rather than found. *Promethium* was named for the Titan who stole fire, because it came out of a nuclear reactor. The elements named after places and people (*polonium, einsteinium*) are Latinized, not Latin, but the *-ium* still marks them as members of the club.

Then there are the suffixes. Sugars end in *-ose* (*sucrose, lactose, fructose*), by analogy with *glucose*, which was from Greek *gleukos*, sweet wine. Enzymes end in *-ase* (*lactase, protease*), which is nothing but the *-ase* of *diastase*, the first enzyme named, chopped off and reused. Alcohols end in *-ol*. Alkaloids end in *-ine*. None of this was designed; each was a single coinage that became a template.

## Biology: named by a committee that spoke Greek

*Protein* was coined in 1838 by the Dutch chemist Gerardus Mulder, on a suggestion from Berzelius, from Greek *proteios*, "of the first rank," because he believed it was the fundamental substance of all living matter. He was more right than Lavoisier.

*Dinosaur* was coined in 1842 by Richard Owen from *deinos* (terrible) + *sauros* (lizard). Dinosaurs are not lizards. *Pterodactyl* is wing-finger. *Tyrannosaurus rex* is tyrant-lizard king, half Greek, half Latin. *Triceratops* is three-horn-face. Paleontologists have been naming things in Greek ever since, partly because Greek compounds sound impressive and partly because the international rules of nomenclature require names to be Latinized, and Greek Latinizes easily.

*Photosynthesis* is light-putting-together. *Chlorophyll* is green-leaf. *Mitochondrion* is thread-granule. *Chromosome* is colored body. The whole of cell biology is named after what things looked like under a nineteenth-century microscope, in the language of a civilization that had never seen a cell.

## Physics: named for amber and going

*Electricity* comes from *elektron*, Greek for amber, because rubbing amber was the ancient world's only known way of producing static. William Gilbert coined *electricus* in 1600. The *electron* was named in 1891 by George Johnstone Stoney, before it was discovered, as the name for the fundamental unit of charge.

*Ion* is Greek for "going," and was suggested to Michael Faraday in the 1830s by William Whewell, a philosopher who was very good at this sort of thing. Whewell also gave us *anode* (way up), *cathode* (way down), *anion, cation*, and the word *scientist* itself, in 1833, by analogy with *artist*. Before Whewell, scientists were "natural philosophers."

*Energy* is Aristotle's *energeia*, repurposed by Thomas Young in 1807. *Entropy* was coined by Rudolf Clausius in 1865 from *en* + *trope* (turning), deliberately made to resemble *energy*. *Photon* is from *phos*, light, with the *-on* of *electron*. *Proton* is "first." *Neutron* is Latin *neuter* with a Greek suffix. *Quark* is from *Finnegans Wake* and has no classical roots at all; Murray Gell-Mann chose it in 1964 as a deliberate break from the tradition that had already produced *hadron* (thick), *lepton* (thin), and *baryon* (heavy). The tradition mostly ignored him.

## Computing: named for pruning and steering

*Computer* is Latin *computare*, *com* + *putare*. *Putare* meant to prune, to clean, and from there to reckon up and to think. (*Reputation* is what people reckon you to be; *dispute* is to reckon apart; *deputy* is one assigned by reckoning.) Until the 1940s a computer was a person, usually a woman, who did calculations.

*Data* is the Latin plural of *datum*, "a thing given." *Digital* is from *digitus*, finger, because you count on them. *Calculator* is from the pebbles. *Algorithm* is not classical at all: it is the name of the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi, Latinized as *algorismus*, and then misspelled under the influence of Greek *arithmos*, number. *Cybernetics* was coined in 1948 by Norbert Wiener from *kybernetes*, steersman. Latin had borrowed that Greek word as *gubernator*, which became *governor*. So *cyber* and *govern* are the same word, which is a little too apt.

## The hybrid problem, and its resolution

For two centuries there was a rule, never enforced but widely felt, that a proper coinage should be all Greek or all Latin. *Television, automobile, sociology, homosexual* (Greek *homo*, same, plus Latin *sexus*; coined in 1869 by Karl-Maria Kertbeny), *bureaucracy, meritocracy* all broke it and, as chapter two mentioned, were sneered at. They all survived. The lesson from the neologism factory is that etymological purity has never once determined whether a word lives; sound and need determine that. The purists were right that the words were mongrels and wrong that it mattered.

# 11. Impostors and the Etymological Fallacy

Two kinds of error remain. The first is seeing a root where there isn't one. The second is thinking that the root, when it is there, settles what the word means. Both deserve a chapter, because both are the failure modes of a person who has just learned what you have just learned.

## Words that look assembled but aren't

**Hamburger** contains no ham. It is a thing from Hamburg. The *-burger* got sawed off and reattached to everything, producing *cheeseburger*, which does contain cheese. **Sparrowgrass** is *asparagus* mangled by people who wanted it to mean something. **Female** is not *fe* + *male*; it is Latin *femella*, little woman, and its spelling was later bent toward *male* by people who assumed the two were related. **Island** has no *isle* in it: it is Old English *igland*, water-land, and the silent *s* was inserted in the sixteenth century by scholars who wrongly assumed it came from Latin *insula* (which is where *isle* comes from). They also stuck a *b* in *debt* and *doubt* to show off the Latin *debitum* and *dubitare*, and a *c* in *scissors* by false analogy with *scindere*. The silent letters in English are frequently scars from etymology done badly.

**Pen** and **pencil** are unrelated. *Pen* is *penna*, feather. *Pencil* is *penicillus*, a little brush, a diminutive of *peniculus*, a little tail, a diminutive of *penis*, which meant tail before it meant anything else. *Penicillin* is the same word, because the mold looks like a little brush.

**Belfry** has nothing to do with bells; it was *berfrey*, a siege tower, and the *l* crept in because bells got hung in them. **Crayfish** is not a fish; it is French *écrevisse*, reinterpreted. **Outrage** is not *out* + *rage*; it is French *outrage*, from Latin *ultra*, going beyond. **Sandblind** does not involve sand; it is Old English *sam-*, half. **Bridegroom** is *bryd* + *guma* (man), and *guma* died, so *groom* moved in. This process, where speakers rebuild an opaque word out of familiar parts, is called folk etymology, and it is the linguistic version of pareidolia: we see faces in the clouds and roots in the syllables.

**Pediatrics** and **pedestrian** share no root, as chapter five warned: Greek *paid-*, child, versus Latin *ped-*, foot. **Homosexual** and **Homo sapiens**: Greek *homos*, same, versus Latin *homo*, man. **Pan** the god and **pan-** the prefix are unrelated. **Antic** and **antique** are the same word, *antiquus*, old; *antic* came through Italian *antico*, which was applied to the grotesque figures found painted in excavated Roman ruins. But **anti-** the prefix is Greek for "against" and has nothing to do with either. And when you see *-ology* on the end of *tautology* and *cosmetology* and *Scientology*, only one of those is the study of anything.

The general rule: a root is confirmed when it appears in several words with related meanings, or when a dictionary says so. A single word that "looks like" it contains a root is a hypothesis, not a finding.

## The etymological fallacy

Now for the more insidious error. Suppose you have learned that *decimate* means "kill one in ten." You then encounter a news story saying that a storm decimated a town, and you feel a small warm glow of superiority: the writer means *devastated*, and does not know it. Was the writer wrong?

No. This is the etymological fallacy: the belief that a word's original meaning is its true meaning, and that later senses are corruptions. It is tempting for exactly the reason roots are useful: the original meaning is often illuminating. But illuminating is not the same as authoritative. Consider what the fallacy commits you to. *Nice* would mean ignorant. *Silly* would mean blessed. *Sinister* would mean left-handed. *Hysterical* would apply only to people with a uterus. A *dilapidated* building would have to be made of stone (*lapis*). You would have to *consider* only by looking at stars and be *sarcastic* only by tearing flesh. Nobody accepts this. So nobody actually believes the etymological fallacy as a principle; they only deploy it selectively, against words whose drift they happened to notice.

The philosophical point underneath is that meaning is not a hidden property of a word that etymology excavates. Meaning is what a word does in the mouths of the people who use it, and it changes when they change. Etymology tells you the history of that use, which is a different and wonderful thing. It tells you *why* the word is spelled as it is, what image it started from, what its relatives are. It gives you a strong prior about what an unfamiliar word means, which is the whole practical justification for this book. But when the prior conflicts with how a word is actually used, the usage is the data and the prior should update. *Decimate* now means to destroy a large part of. It has meant that in English since the seventeenth century, nearly as long as the word has existed in English at all, because the one-in-ten sense was mostly a historical term for a Roman practice, and English speakers rarely needed a word for that.

*Enormity* is a live version of the same fight. It comes from *e norma*, out of the norm, and originally meant a monstrous wickedness. Many people now use it to mean enormous size. The usage guides say this is an error; the corpus says it is at least as common as the old one. Give it fifty years.

The right attitude, I think, is this: learn the roots, enjoy the roots, use the roots to guess. Then let the guess be a guess. Anyone who tells you the "real" meaning of a word is what it meant in Latin is telling you that the past has authority over the present, and the whole history of English is a demonstration that it doesn't.

# 12. A Method for Unknown Words

Everything so far has been the kit. This chapter is the procedure for using it, followed by some worked examples, followed by advice on how to actually retain any of this.

## The procedure

When you hit an unfamiliar classical-looking word:

1. **Peel the suffix.** Identify the ending and what part of speech it marks. *-tion, -ment, -ity, -ance* make nouns. *-ous, -ive, -al, -ic, -able* make adjectives. *-ize, -ify, -ate* make verbs. *-ology, -itis, -cracy, -phobia* carry their own meanings.
2. **Peel the prefix.** Check the first syllable against the prefix list in the appendix. Watch for the assimilated forms: a doubled consonant after an initial vowel is a strong hint.
3. **What's left is the root.** Match it against something you know. Allow for vowel changes (*cap/cip/cept*) and consonant changes (*fac/fect*).
4. **Assemble a literal meaning.** Put the parts back together in plain English, not left to right but root first, then prefix, then suffix.
5. **Check it against context.** The literal meaning is a prior. The sentence the word appears in is the evidence. If they agree, you are done. If they don't, drift has happened; use the context and look the word up later.

## Worked examples

**Circumlocution.** *Circum* (around) + *locut* (spoken, from *loqui*, the root of *eloquent, colloquial, ventriloquist, soliloquy*) + *-ion* (noun). "Speaking around." Talking around the point. That is exactly what it means.

**Hypercholesterolemia.** *Hyper* (over) + *cholesterol* + *-emia* (blood condition). Too much cholesterol in the blood. Twenty letters, three parts, no memorization.

**Antidisestablishmentarianism.** The famous one. Working from the inside out: *stabl* (from *stabilis*, steady, from *stare*, stand) plus an *e-* that French added to ease pronunciation gives *establish*, to set up. *Dis-* undoes it: *disestablish*, to remove the official status of, specifically of the Church of England as the state church. *-ment* makes a noun; *-arian* makes a person who holds a position; *-ism* makes the position. *Anti-* opposes it. So: the ideology of those who oppose the removal of the Church of England's official status. The pieces are real nineteenth-century political vocabulary; the full word is mostly a twentieth-century showpiece. But it is entirely computable.

**Defenestration.** *De* (down, out of) + *fenestra* (window) + *-ation*. Throwing someone out a window. It has a Latin root, a French cousin (*fenêtre*), and a specific historical referent: the Defenestration of Prague in 1618, when Protestant nobles threw two imperial regents and their secretary out of a castle window, starting the Thirty Years' War. All three survived, having landed in a dung heap, which Catholic accounts attributed to angels and Protestant accounts to the dung heap.

**Pneumonoultramicroscopicsilicovolcanoconiosis.** Forty-five letters, coined in 1935 as a joke, and included in some dictionaries as a lung disease from inhaling fine volcanic silica dust. It is nonetheless perfectly parsable: *pneumono* (lung) + *ultra* (beyond) + *microscopic* (small-seeing) + *silico* (silica) + *volcano* + *coni* (dust, from Greek *konis*) + *-osis* (condition). The parts are honest even when the word is a stunt.

**Obsequious.** *Ob* (toward) + *sequ* (follow; as in *sequence, consequence, subsequent, pursue*, and *sect*, a following) + *-ious*. "Following after." Fawning. The picture of a courtier trailing behind a lord is right there in the word.

**Recalcitrant.** *Re* (back) + *calcitrare* (to kick, from *calx*, heel) + *-ant*. Kicking back with the heels, like a mule. The word means stubbornly resistant, and the mule is still in it.

**Supercilious.** *Super* (above) + *cilium* (eyelid; *supercilium* is the eyebrow). Eyebrow-raising. Haughty. The gesture became the adjective.

**Preposterous.** *Prae* (before) + *posterus* (coming after). Before-behind, back-to-front. Absurd. This is the word's actual etymology and also a fair description of how it sounds.

**Nostalgia.** *Nostos* (homecoming; the *Odyssey* is a *nostos* story) + *algia* (pain). It was coined in 1688 by a Swiss medical student, Johannes Hofer, as a diagnosis for homesick mercenaries. It was considered a potentially fatal disease. Now it sells vinyl records.

**Idiot.** Greek *idiotes*, a private person: one who held no office, or a layman as opposed to an expert, from *idios*, one's own (as in *idiosyncrasy* and *idiom*, one's own way of speaking). It was not an insult in Athens. The slide from "private citizen" to "layman" to "ignorant" to "stupid" took about two thousand years, and every step was a small judgment about people who don't know what the speaker knows.

## How to retain it

You will not retain it by reading this book once. Nobody would. Here is what works.

**Learn roots, not words.** The compression argument from chapter one is the whole strategy. Forty-odd prefixes, a hundred and fifty roots, and a couple dozen suffixes is a few hundred items, and it unlocks tens of thousands of words. That is a better deal than any vocabulary list.

**Use spaced repetition, but put the family on the card, not the definition.** If you make flashcards, the front should be the root (*spec*) and the back should be the meaning plus four or five words that use it, ideally ones you already know. The point is to build a hook between the abstract chunk and concrete words that already live in your head. The appendix is formatted to make this easy.

**Notice, then look up.** The habit that matters is pausing at an unfamiliar word for five seconds to try the decomposition before reaching for a dictionary. When you then look it up, you get a small hit of either confirmation or surprise, and both stick better than a definition read cold. Lookup is cheap: the Online Etymology Dictionary, Wiktionary, and the etymology notes in any good dictionary are all fine.

**Read things with lots of the tail in them.** Legal writing, medical writing, old philosophy, nineteenth-century novels, and scientific abstracts are dense with classical vocabulary. You will meet the kit constantly, in context, which is the only place vocabulary really settles.

And finally: enjoy the drift. The roots are a map of where the words came from, not a fence around where they can go. The best part of knowing them is not correctness. It is that ordinary sentences start to shimmer a little, with candidates in white togas and muscles like mice and companions sharing bread, and once you have seen that, you cannot unsee it, and I don't think you would want to.
